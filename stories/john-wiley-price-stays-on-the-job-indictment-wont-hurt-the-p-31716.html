John Wiley Price Stays on the Job; Indictment Won’t Hurt the Power he Holds in Dallas Right Now

July 28, 2014 Black

File 2011/Staff Photo
Commissioner John Wiley Price, seen in his office at the Dallas County Administration Building, was back at work just hours after his arrest.

Any notion that corruption charges might immediately end John Wiley Price’s career quickly disappeared last week: Just hours after the county commissioner was arrested, he went to the office.

He’s expected to show up again Monday. And his colleagues on the Commissioners Court expect they’ll see him in his seat at the court’s next regular meeting Aug. 5.

County Republicans vow to push for commissioners to nudge Price to resign. It’s unlikely the court, dominated by Democrats, would do so, and there’s no apparent mechanism to strip any of his authority.

“At this point, it’s just an indictment,” County Administrator Darryl Martin said. “More than likely, it’s business as usual.”

Given the presumption of innocence and the years it may take for his complex federal bribery and tax evasion case to wind through the courts, Price’s seat is probably safe for a while.

His power and influence, though, may be another matter.

During his three decades in office, Price has established himself as one of the most powerful politicians in the region and the person who knows — and controls — county government better than anyone.

‘Let the system work’

Billed as “Our Man Downtown,” he has repeatedly cruised to re-election to represent the southeastern part of Dallas County. He’s exerted great influence over local Democratic politics, too.

In interviews and statements released after the arrest, none of Price’s four colleagues on the court mentioned resignation. Several said that they didn’t immediately see a need to change operations — even as the government alleges that Price improperly influenced county contracts for personal gain.

“If any corruption does exist, then justice should take its course,” said Commissioner Mike Cantrell, the lone Republican on the court. “The process has been working over the past number of years, and it has culminated in an indictment. Let’s just let the system work.”

Meanwhile, Price will continue to have a vote on the Commissioners Court. That will give him an official say on budgetary issues, contract approval and personnel matters.

Wade Emmert, chairman of the Dallas County Republican Party, said Price should resign. If that doesn’t happen, Emmert said, he plans to speak at the next Commissioners Court meeting and demand that Price’s colleagues pass a resolution calling for his exit.

“He’s innocent until proven guilty, but I don’t know how this Commissioners Court could in good conscience allow him to continue to serve in a capacity that he has already betrayed,” Emmert said.

Such a resolution wouldn’t have teeth even if it was approved. An indictment without a conviction isn’t enough to remove him from office, Emmert acknowledged. Regardless, the gesture seems unlikely.
