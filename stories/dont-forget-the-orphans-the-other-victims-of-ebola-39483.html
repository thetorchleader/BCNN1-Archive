Don’t Forget the Orphans — the Other Victims of Ebola

October 14, 2014 Opinion

Emmanuel Junior Cooper of Monrovia, Liberia, lost his parents to Ebola (Photo: Jerome Delay, AP)

by Dennis Rosen

President Obama’s decision last month to send 3,000 U.S. troops to West Africa to help contain the Ebola epidemic that has killed more than 4,000 people is perhaps the first real effort by the international community to seriously address the outbreak after months of inaction.Although attention in this country is currently focused on a Dallas hospital nurse who became infected after helping care for a Liberian patient who died from Ebola last week, it is important to stay focused on the devastation it is causing in West Africa

Ebola kills about half of those it infects. Transmitted through bodily fluid, and therefore less infective than airborne pathogens such as measles or the flu, it has nonetheless resulted in widespread social upheaval and panic in Liberia and Sierra Leone as people scramble to protect themselves and their families.

One of the terrible consequences of this epidemic has been the high number of children who, after having been orphaned from their parents, have been ostracized because of fear that they may transmit the disease to whomever takes them in. The West and Central African regional director for UNICEF, Manuel Fontaine, recently estimated the number of children orphaned by Ebola as of Sept. 30 to be at least 3,700. A recent report by the BBC described children being “discovered alone in the hospitals where their parents had died or in their communities (and) where, if they were lucky, they were being fed by neighbors.”

With the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention projecting that the number of people infected by Ebola in West Africa could reach 1.4 million by January 2015, the wide-scale orphaning, stigmatization and abandonment of hundreds of thousands of children could be disastrous and have far-reaching repercussions.
