Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church In Pennsylvania Holds “Save Our Sons Sunday” In Wake of Michael Brown Shooting

August 29, 2014 Black

Speakers at Bethel AME Church of Ardmore’s Save Our Sons Sunday included Rev. Carolyn Cavaness, pastor, center, with Donald Jackson of Ardmore, right, and Marvin Richardson, left, with son Noah, 4.

Even before the calls for change that were interwoven with mourning for Michael Brown at his funeral Monday, a church hundreds of miles away in Ardmore stood ready to respond.

Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church dedicated its worship service last week as Save Our Sons Sunday. Speakers reflected on the turmoil that has followed the shooting death of the 18-year-old Brown at the hands of a white police officer in Ferguson, Mo., Aug. 9 – an incident that has reawakened disturbing questions about race relations in America that had quieted but not been stilled since the killing of another African-American teenager, Trayvon Martin, in Florida in 2012.

The church’s pastor, Rev. Carolyn Cavaness, also addressed the challenges of moving forward in a passionate sermon whose profound message was captured in two word, repeated in a compelling refrain: “Choose life.”

Cavaness, who came to Bethel AME in May as the first woman pastor in the church’s nearly 120-year history, was moved to focus on the issues raised by protests and police response in Ferguson to “lift up our boys and men in a very special way.” Sunday’s service was meant, she said, to be a time of healing from the pain of the last weeks, but also to inspire a new commitment to fostering the safety and well-being of young black men.

At Bethel, “We come from a heritage and a legacy that speaks for those who do not have [a] voice” and goes “the extra mile,” she said. “As differences seek to rule the day, we search for another path, a new way to live in community,” read an opening prayer.

Church member Donald Jackson, who grew up in Ardmore, graduating from Lower Merion High School in 1957, said his “life’s work” has been with youth in Philadelphia, including delinquent youth. In that way, “I have met many Michael Browns,” he said – potential victims of what he sees as a structure in America that “uses racism, classism and sexism” to control power and set groups apart.

There are other forces at work, Jackson said. Seeing many of the young men he was worked with in prison now, he said those include “a prison industrial complex” driven by profit to “fill beds and cells.” But there are also, among some youth, “Attitudes that ‘I may disrespect you but you must respect me’ ” that can be “as dangerous as anger.”

“How do we help our young men negotiate a culture” in which they have a “burning natural desire to fit in,” but “are purposely perceived as dangerous by some in the larger society?” he mused. “Though we may not know how, we do have the will” to find answers, he affirmed.
