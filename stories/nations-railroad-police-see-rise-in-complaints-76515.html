Nation’s Railroad Police See Rise in Complaints

May 29, 2015 Black

© Ryan Stone for The New York Times Edduard Prince was charged with trespassing in 2013 after crossing tracks. States have been lax in oversight of the railroad police.

Since the days when Jesse James robbed trains in the Old West, obscure police forces run by the nation’s railroads have operated with the power to make arrests, issue warrants and perform undercover work.

These railroad police officers, who have been licensed by states, have been accused of physical assaults, racial profiling and harassment of railroad employees, according to lawsuits, interviews and a review of court records by The New York Times. The records and interviews show that there have been numerous complaints and dozens of lawsuits filed against railroad police officers in recent years.

While police departments across the country face increasing scrutiny and demand for reform after several cases of brutality, the railroad authorities appear to operate with near impunity.

“There needs to be better oversight of these corporate police forces, because right now it is sorely lacking,” said Lloyd C. Larsen, a Republican state representative in Wyoming, one of only two states that do not allow the railroad police to have law enforcement authority; Minnesota is the other. “No one knows what they do or how they operate outside of people at the company.”

Since 2009, the railroad police have arrested more than 300 residents in Overtown, a predominantly black neighborhood in Miami, on charges of trespassing. Nearly 90 percent of those charges have been dismissed.

In Oklahoma in 2010, a Union Pacific railroad police officer was captured on video choking a woman he accused of trespassing and resisting arrest. A local court dropped the charges against the woman and she settled a lawsuit with the company earlier this year.

Two former employees have recently brought civil lawsuits against the Canadian National Railway, claiming that the railroad police failed to properly investigate their claims. In one of the suits in Illinois, a man claimed he was attacked by co-workers, and in the other, a woman in Michigan who worked for the railroad said that she was sexually assaulted on the job.

Of the eight largest private railroads that were contacted by The Times, only two — Burlington Northern Santa Fe and Union Pacific — would provide crime statistics. They gave information only on arrests on railroad property, not on complaints or disciplinary actions against their police officers.

Federal authorities have ceded oversight of the railroad police to the states, and few states, if any, keep data of complaints against the railroad police.

People who say they have been mistreated by the railroad police have little recourse except to complain to the railroads, but because the railroads are private companies, they can operate largely in secret.

Amtrak, the nation’s largest passenger rail line, is an exception because it is subject to oversight by Congress and to federal open-records laws as a government-funded corporation.

To be sure, most of the railroad police officers carry out their jobs without incident, protecting more than $515 billion of goods carried by trains each year.

“Just like any police force, there will be people who bend the rules,” said Ed Greenberg, a spokesman for the American Association of Railroads, a trade group. “But complaints about their police forces are handled by railroads in a professional manner.”
