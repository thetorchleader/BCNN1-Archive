Documentary to Examine the Role Black Landowners and Farmers in Mississippi Played in Getting the Voting Rights Act Passed

February 17, 2015 Black

Robert Clarke Jr., who came to own the land on which his great-grandparents were enslaved and became the first black candidate elected to a statewide office in Mississippi since Reconstruction, is interviewed for a documentary.
(Photo: Special to The Clarion-Ledger)

In honor of the 50th anniversary of the passage of the 1965 Voting Rights Act, the Smithsonian Channel is airing a two-part documentary examining the role played by black landowners and farmers as a real driving force behind the passage of the legislation.

Narrated by actor Danny Glover, the first part, “Mississippi Inferno: Seeds of Revolt” airs at 7 tonight. The second part, “Deeds of Defiance,” follows.

During Freedom Summer of 1964, less than 6 percent of blacks in Mississippi were registered to vote, the lowest figure in the country. The documentary examines the self- efficiency that black landowners managed to operate under that allowed them to defy the de jure laws of repression.

Black churches were built on land owned by black farmers, and their status in the community allowed for more autonomy.

Holmes County farmer T.C. Johnson’s daughter, Sylvia Montgomery, recalls her father: “He would receive less repercussions than some of the other persons that did not own land. He was freer, so to speak.”

Much of the focus of the documentary features the work of black landowners in Holmes County. Men like Jody Saffold, Griffin McLaurin and Edmond Clark risked their lives by housing Freedom Summer volunteers.
