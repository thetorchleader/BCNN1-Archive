Second ‘Blood Moon’ of 2014 to Appear in the Sky Early Wednesday Morning

October 7, 2014 Second Coming Watch

Early risers across much of the world are in for a treat on Wednesday morning – a total lunar eclipse, also known as a “blood moon” due to the coppery, reddish color the moon takes as it passes into Earth’s shadow.

The total eclipse is the second of four over a two-year period that began April 15 and concludes on Sept. 28, 2015. The so-called tetrad is unusual because the full eclipses are visible in all or parts of the United States, according to retired NASA astrophysicist Fred Espenak.

Weather permitting, Wednesday’s eclipse should be visible to skywatchers in North America, Australia, western South America and parts of East Asia. The eclipse should reach totality just before sunrise, at 6:25 a.m. EDT (1025 GMT).

If cloudy skies are a problem, both NASA.gov and Slooh.com will be hosting live webcasts. NASA’s begins at 3 a.m. EDT and Slooh’s at 5 a.m. EDT.

An eclipse occurs when the moon passes through Earth’s shadow, called the umbra.

As for the reddish hue, Tony Phillips, an astronomer with SpaceWeather.com, says to imagine yourself on the moon: “Overhead hangs Earth, nightside down, completely hiding the sun behind it. The eclipse is under way. You might expect Earth to be utterly dark, but … the rim of the planet is on fire.”

“You’re seeing every sunrise and every sunset in the world, all at once,” Phillips wrote in an article on NASA’s science website.

The light beams into Earth’s shadow, filling it with a coppery glow that colors the moon red, he said.

The entire eclipse will be visible from the Pacific Ocean, regions immediately bordering it and the northwestern section of North America. Farther east, the later phases of the eclipse occur after moonset. The eclipse will not be visible from Europe, Africa and the Middle East, NASA said.

(Reporting by Irene Klotz in Portland, Maine; editing by G Crosse)

The moon turns an orange-red hue during a total lunar eclipse on April 14, 2014. Another total eclipse is expected on Wednesday morning.

The “blood moon” will be coming back for the second time this year, just in time for Halloween preparations, as the Earth is on course to pass directly between the moon and the sun early Wednesday morning.

In the latest chapter of a busy lunar year that has already included three consecutive supermoons and another total lunar eclipse, the moon will adopt a reddish glow Wednesday morning as the Earth, sun and moon align, according to the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette.

Referred to as a “Hunter’s Moon” or “blood moon,” the lunar eclipse will only be visible to about half the world at the time the celestial bodies align and the moon is engulfed in the Earth’s shadow, according to NBC News. North America is one of the lucky continents with a front-row seat.

The total eclipse will be visible at 6:25 a.m. EDT, though lunar anomalies are expected to be seen as early as 4:15 a.m. EDT, when the moon passes into the outer edges of Earth’s shadow, according to USA Today and Space.com.

“The Earth casts a pretty large shadow, so you’re going to see it pretty much wherever you are,” said Brendan Mulligan, director of Buhl Planetarium & Observatory at Carnegie Science Center, according to the Post-Gazette. “As long as conditions are right … As long as it’s nice out, not too humid, not too cloudy.”

Slivers of light from the sun are still able to refract around the Earth to the moon, giving the lunar body a temporary reddish-orange, sunset-like hue. The total eclipse is expected to only last a matter of minutes, but the reddish glow should be visible hours before and hours after total alignment, according to Space.com.

“You see the Earth’s shadow start to gobble up more and more of the moon. And as time passes into [eclipse] totality, the moon actually turns red,” said Mulligan, according to the Post-Gazette.

Hawaii and much of the West Coast will be able to see the entire five-hour process, starting at 1:15 a.m. PDT. But sunrise in other American time zones will cut the eclipse visibility short. The eclipse is not expected to be visible in the Eastern Time Zone long after sunrise.
