50 Years After Bloody Sunday, Selma, Alabama Is Still Struggling

March 4, 2015 Black

Fifty years after Bloody Sunday, Selma residents still struggle with social segregation, crime and a lack of jobs.
Connor Sheets | csheets@al.com

Running northwest from the iconic Edmund Pettus Bridge, Broad Street in Selma’s historic downtown looks much as it did 50 years ago when the city became ground zero in the American civil rights movement.

Decades-old neon signs protrude from the fading facades of locally owned eateries, shops and businesses, a number of which have been in operation since before Bloody Sunday put Selma on the national map.

But a lot has changed since then. Willie Hains was born in Selma 59 years ago, and he still retains vivid memories of a time when he and other black residents were treated as second-class citizens in nearly every aspect of their lives.

“It really done got a whole heap of better. I remember the day when we’d walk on this street here and if three white people came down the street you had to get way over when we should have been sharing the street,” he said, standing under a Broad Street awning shielding him and a friend from a steady downpour. “You had to not share the street, you had to get out of the street. So it’s way better.”

But Hains and many other black Selma natives emphasized that there’s still much to be done to achieve the dream of a prosperous Selma that provides equal opportunities for all its people.

“We need more jobs and crime needs to get better, because if the crime doesn’t get better, then no jobs are going to ever come here,” said Tony Hall, a 38-year-old black Selma resident who does landscaping, repairs and other work at Selma City Hall.

This weekend President Barack Obama and former President George W. Bush are scheduled to meet in this small city to commemorate an important milestone in this nation’s history.
