Shorter AME Church in Morton, Delaware Celebrate 125 Years of History, Family, and Faith

March 16, 2014 Uncategorized

From left are Marian Jennings, Elaine Phillips and Pastor Roselin Martin of Shorter AME Church in Morton, which is celebrating its 125th anniversary March 21-23. Jennings and Phillips have been members of the church for more than 60 years. (Times Staff / JULIA WILKINSON)

When Edward Lytle moved to Morton from Pennsylvania’s upstate mining country in the late 1800s, he was a builder with a coal and ice delivery company. Once in the Delaware County community, he and his wife, Mary, added a laundry business to their enterprises and kept horses while raising a house full of children.

But Lytle’s longest lasting legacy was borne of another hat he wore, that of a Methodist minister. It now stands in the form of Shorter African Methodist Episcopal Church on the 100 block of Pennington Avenue.

“They started the church in his home and they kept it up there until outgrowing the space,” said one of the Lytles’ grandchildren, Marian Jennings, who still lives in Morton and will be 86 in August.

While her grandfather was never pastor of the church, he and Jennings’ paternal grandmother hosted the congregation’s first services in the Victorian house they built at 254 Pennington Ave., starting in 1889, seven years before Morton was incorporated as a borough from Springfield Township.

“The people of color living in Morton at the time attended Kedron Methodist Church. Many people were beginning to move in from Maryland and Delaware, and they wanted their own church,” said church historian Lisa Lockley, a Widener University Child Development Center teacher who has been a member of Shorter A.M.E. all of her 57 years.

The Morton resident noted that fewer than 30 years had passed since President Abraham Lincoln’s Emancipation Proclamation declaring that slaves in the South should be freed, when the new African-American congregation was formed in the Lytles’ living room.

“For me, I always wondered, ‘My God, some of these people could have been slaves,’” said Lockley. “It’s almost like Shorter became a united front for the race itself.”

In honor of the 125th anniversary of the founding of the Morton church, named for the ninth consecrated bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, the Rt. Rev. James A. Shorter, the congregation will be hosting festivities this Friday through Sunday.

They will start 7 p.m. Friday with a revival at the intimate, flagstone church where the Rev. Michael Heath, pastor of Living Waters Church in Collingdale, will preach and The Voices of nearby First Baptist Church of Morton will sing. Shorter’s own male chorus — including the church’s oldest member, 92-year-old Frank Single — will sing, as will the Delco Mass Choir 3 p.m. Saturday when Lockley’s documentary about the church will also be screened. The celebratory weekend will end Sunday at Shorter A.M.E. with an 11 a.m. sermon by the Rev. A. L. Stephans, who was pastor there from 1969 to 1979, and a 3:30 p.m. service featuring former Shorter member, the Rev. Stephen Lewis, now pastor of Bethel A.M.E. in Freeport, N.Y., and the Kingdom Empowerment Ministries Choir. A display featuring Shorter’s history will be on exhibit in the church basement.

Shorter was established 73 years after Richard Allen, a Philadelphian who purchased his freedom from a Delaware slave owner, established the African Methodist Episcopal Church as the first national black church in the United States. He became a Methodist at age 17 after hearing a white itinerant Methodist minister passionately preach against slavery. In 1816, Allen established the first A.M.E. church, Mother Bethel, in an old blacksmith shop at 6th and Lombard streets in Philadelphia. The denomination now has more than 2.5 million members nationwide.
