Rev. Jeremiah Wright Jr., Discusses the State of the Black Church at Aenon Baptist Church in Rochester, NY

April 25, 2014 African-American

The Rev. Jeremiah Wright delivers his talk, African Centered Theology in an Age of Color Blindness, at the lecture series.
(Photo: SHAWN DOWD/staff photographer )

The Rev. Jeremiah Wright Jr., the pastor thrust into the national media spotlight for controversial comments made during President Barack Obama’s 2008 campaign, addressed a packed house at Aenon Baptist Church in Rochester on Thursday to discuss the state of the African-American church in 2014.

The speech was part of the Colgate Rochester Crozier Divinity School’s African-American Legacy Lecture series.

Those expecting verbal fireworks during Wright’s address, titled “African Centered Theology in an Age of Color Blindness: They Forgot Their Story,” were left wanting.

His focused message drew on the work of several historical authors such as Carter G. Woodson and Chancellor Williams as he weaved through the minefield of race, theology and African and African-American history.

Wright asked whether the current African-American church will turn its collective back on Africa as it did over a century ago.

“People who forget their own story are a dead people,” Wright reminded the more than 300 people gathered in Aenon’s pews.

Wright said Africans landing on the South Carolina and Georgia coasts brought their theologies, and with blacks who were not scarred by the Middle Passage, helped create the African-centered theology and the religious tradition that has kept blacks alive through oppression since the founding of the first black church in America in 1787.

He added that the black church principles of liberation and transformation are rooted in the biblical chapters of Isaiah 61 and Luke 4, as preached by Jesus.

Wright is urging today’s black church leaders to retain their focus on racial accomplishment while embracing and forwarding a theology of liberation and fairness that can cross racial lines.

“It was preached to set free those who were held in bondage, spiritually, psychologically, and sometimes physically,” Wright said. “And it was practiced to set the slave-holders free from the notion that they could define other human beings or confine them. Their souls (were) set free by the power of the gospel.”

Wright said the continuance of African-American theology, within its definition, “means no one race stops being who they are as God created them,” and “… no one race is privileged over some other race.”
