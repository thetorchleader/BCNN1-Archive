Recognition Danger Faces Plainclothes Police Officers in New York

May 4, 2015 Black

© Chang W. Lee/The New York Times Crime scene investigators set up a tent outside a home on 212th Place in Queens Village, where Demetrius Blackwell was arrested in the shooting of an officer.

For most city residents, the New York Police Department’s elite anticrime officers are invisible observers, prowling the streets in unmarked cars and civilian clothes in a bid to stop felony crimes before they start. 

But they do not go unseen by those who have previously attracted their attention. For those people, like Demetrius Blackwell, who shot and critically wounded an anticrime officer in Queens on Saturday, they are quickly recognized as the officers whose job it is, in part, to chase down people carrying guns.

That obligation, even at a time of historically low crime, puts the department’s anticrime unit at an elevated risk. The three officers who have been shot this year — Officer Brian Moore, who was in critical condition after the shooting on Saturday, and two officers who were wounded responding to a robbery in the Bronx in January — belonged to plainclothes anticrime teams.

“When you’re grabbing a guy who has a gun, who’s wanted, he might not want to go back to jail,” said Michael Reiter, a retired New York police detective who patrolled East Flatbush, Brooklyn, with a unit in the 1980s. “They’d rather shoot it out.”

Mr. Blackwell, who has served time in state prison for attempted murder, appeared to make that calculation on Saturday. Officer Moore, 25, and his partner, Erik Jansen, 30, were on the lookout for people surveying homes in preparation for a burglary. They noticed Mr. Blackwell adjusting his waistband, and before they could get out of the car, Mr. Blackwell fired at them and ran, the police said.

Anticrime officers’ mandate, to stop those they believe are acting suspiciously, has angered some residents, who say the stops are racially motivated, allowing officers to harass people in high-crime neighborhoods.

But the episode on Saturday appeared to be the kind of close reading of mannerisms and movements that anticrime officers pride themselves on. Policing experts say anticrime officers study the physical cues presented by someone who has a gun. They learn blending techniques, like picking clothes that are common within a certain neighborhood.

“We would look at eye contact,” Mr. Reiter recalled. “We would look at if somebody was nervous, if somebody in a cab would duck down or they’d move their hands.”

When suspects became too familiar with him, Mr. Reiter said, he occasionally drove his father’s livery cab as a disguise. “You reinvented yourself,” he said.

Ideally, Eugene O’Donnell, a professor at the John Jay College of Criminal Justice, said, that kind of street surveillance is driven by the patterns that emerge out of crime complaints in particular locations.

Joseph L. Giacalone, a former detective sergeant who also teaches at the college, said he used to describe where robberies and burglaries were happening to anticrime officers. Using a wide network of street informers and an intimacy with “all the nooks and crannies in a neighborhood,” he said, those officers then tried to anticipate future crimes. “They’re supposed to get the guy before someone calls 911,” Mr. Giacalone said.

The tactics can draw complaints. A citywide version of the precinct-based anticrime teams, called the Street Crime Unit, faced withering criticism in the late 1990s for using overly confrontational methods to catch suspects. A 1999 investigation of the department’s stop-and-frisk practices by the state attorney general found that officers in the Street Crime Unit were much more likely to stop blacks and Hispanics than whites.
