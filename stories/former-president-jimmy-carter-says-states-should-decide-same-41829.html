Former President Jimmy Carter Says States Should Decide Same-Sex Marriage Issue, Not the Federal Government

October 26, 2014 National

Former President Jimmy Carter was a guest on Inside Texas Politics in October, 2014.
(Photo: WFAA)

Former President Jimmy Carter said legalizing same-sex marriage should be left up to the states rather than mandated by the federal government.

“I’m kind of inclined to let the states decide individually,” Carter said.

Carter, 90, has expressed support for gay marriage before, but his comments now on letting the states decide appear to contrast with a 2013 interview with CNN in which the 39th president said that same-sex marriage should be legal nationwide.

“As you see, more and more states are deciding on gay marriage every year. If Texas doesn’t want to have gay marriage, then I think that’s a right for Texas people to decide,” the former president said.

Carter, the first evangelical Christian elected president, said, “I don’t think that the government ought to ever have the right to tell a church to marry people if the church doesn’t want to.

“I’m a Baptist, and the congregation of our church will decide whether we have a man or a woman as pastor, and whether we’ll marry gay people or not.”

On Saturday, the U.S. Department of Justice announced it will recognize same-sex marriages in six new states — Alaska, Arizona, Idaho, North Carolina, West Virginia and Wyoming — bringing to 32 states, plus the District of Columbia, where such marriages are legal.

The decision means gay couples in those states will be able to receive federal benefits, including Social Security benefits for widowers and the ability to file a joint income tax return. The Justice Department’s move comes after the U. S. Supreme Court declined to hear appeals in cases that made gay marriage legal in those states.

In Texas, a federal judge ruled in February that the state’s constitutional amendment defining marriage between a man and woman is unconstitutional. But Republican Gov. Rick Perry and Attorney General Greg Abbott — who is running to replace him — are challenging the ruling in the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals in New Orleans.

In the wide-ranging interview, Carter also said the United States should not send ground troops to fight Islamic State militants in Iraq and Syria.

These days, the former president said, he still hunts on his farm in Plains, Ga., and spends one week every year building houses for Habitat for Humanity. Since joining the organization in the mid-1980s, Carter has helped build or repair 3,800 homes — including more than 100 in Dallas and Fort Worth this month.

As for his own legacy: “I think the two words I would like to have describe me was, I kept the peace and I did all I could to promote human rights,” Carter said. “When I was in the White House … we never fired a bullet; we never dropped a bomb; we never launched a missile.”
