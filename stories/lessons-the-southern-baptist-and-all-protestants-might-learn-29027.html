Lessons the Southern Baptist and All Protestants Might Learn as to Why the Millennium Generation Doesn’t Go to Church

Date not recorded in the original archive Christian

Dr. Amos C. Brown

The Southern Baptist in its National Convention this past June have much to say about seven year decline in baptism in the almost conspicuous absence of young people, 18-29 from the pew. While there are no simple answers to social and religious trends there might be some answers to the Southern Baptist’s frustration that they would consider another point of view on the role of the church.

The role of the protestant church is being challenged by a millennial generation that no longer automatically accepts status quo religious teachings. Even the success of “mega churches” has not allowed contemporary young adults to accept institutional religious organizations that focus more on doctrine and theology rather than actions and works. Far too many churches concern themselves with saving souls for eternity, certainly a noble and necessary mission, at the expense of making lives in the present strong and equitable. The question must be: is the church working to provide healthy, educated, livable conditions for all people in the society? Is the church giving concrete actions to Christ’s dictates to care for the needy not just theologically but with quality housing, quality food, quality education, quality environmental services, quality health care, quality jobs, and quality childcare? Pope Francis explains, “I see the church as a field hospital after battle. It is useless to ask a seriously injured person if he has high cholesterol and about the level of his blood sugars. You have to heal his wounds. Then we can talk about everything else.”

It is certainly the church’s mission to give inspiration and hope to those who face depression and hopelessness; it is certainly the role of the church to provide stimulating and creative worship services that praise the Lord and encourage parishioners; it is certainly the duty of the church to impart the Savior’s message of salvation and faith. “Faith without works is dead faith…and works alone do not give salvation…faith should lead to works.”

If the church is to be an effective institution, it must use its power to engage in effective works rather than judgmental discourse. No longer can church leaders resign themselves to condemn those they consider “different” and “sinful” because of their personal decision about lifestyles. The old “fire and brimstone” punishment for gays, lesbians, bisexuals, and transgenders belittle the major issues facing our society. Relegating women to second-class citizenship in the church highlights the pettiness that many young people use as criticism against religious institutions. Focusing on ceremony over substance in worship services demonstrates the lack of emphasis on the true teachings of Christ: “worship the Lord God with all your heart, soul and MIND” and “love your neighbor as yourself.” True love forces one to make sure the neighbor’s physical as well as spiritual needs are met. The controversial theologian/politician, Adam Clayton Powell, Jr., advocated to his Abyssinian Church parishioners a “not just a Sunday-go-to-meeting church but a seven-day twenty-four hour church. … The heaven or hell to which one goes is right here in the span of years we spend in this body on this earth.” Our time on earth is too short to waste on frivolous dogmatic exercises that neglect the substantive needs of a suffering people. The work is hard and demands more than verbal recrimination against those who appear different. Vigilance and tangible dedication speak to Christ’s mission for his church if others are to follow. Presbyterian minister Maltbie D. Babcock wrote his famous hymn “Be Strong” as an anthem for service to others:

Be strong?
We are not here to play, to dream, to drift;
We have hard work to do and loads to lift;
Shun not the struggle, face it, ‘tis God’s gift.
Be strong, be strong!

Be strong!
Say not the days are evil – who’s to blame?
Stand up, speak out, and bravely, in God’s name
Be strong, be strong!

Be strong!
It matters not how deep entrench’d the wrong,
How hard the battle goes, the day, how long;
Faint not, fight on! Tomorrow comes the song.
Be strong, be strong!

Dr. Amos C. Brown, Chairman of Civil Rights and Social Justice, National Baptist Convention
