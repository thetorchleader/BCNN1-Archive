Law Enforcement Official Eddie Edwards Tells of his Long Journey from Georgia to New Hampshire

March 29, 2015 Black

Eddie Edwards, director of community relations for U.S. Rep. Frank Guinta, R-N.H., at his office in Manchester last week. (Thomas Roy/Union Leader)

Just as his law enforcement career in New Hampshire was reaching new heights, Eddie Edwards’ past in Georgia came rushing back with a telephone call 10 years ago.

His mother was on the line. She asked Edwards, who months earlier became chief of the New Hampshire Liquor Authority’s Enforcement Division, to return to Atlanta and testify on behalf of his older brother, Willie.

Willie had killed a man, and Edwards’ mother wanted him to say that it wasn’t all Willie’s fault because of the abuse the brothers had shared at the hands of their drug-addicted father, Willie Sr., who whipped the boys with electrical cords and beat their mother, Doris, when he was high and angry.

Recalling the happy boy his older brother was before choosing the path presented by their abusive father, and the decisions Edwards made to take a different road, he gave a difficult answer.

“I didn’t do it. I refused,” he said.

Edwards, now 47, said his brother had the same choice: Be like their father or take an alternate route. Willie admired their dad and wanted to be just like him, Edwards said.

“He went full force into that lifestyle,” Edwards said.

Willie started bringing knives to school, then guns. He joined a gang and committed robberies, then did an eight-year stretch in prison for attempted murder, Edwards said. Not long after being released, Willie committed the murder that has him serving 15 years to life at Dooly State Prison in Unadilla, Ga., with a possibility for parole in two years.

“When you’re shaped like that, you have some choices,” Edwards said. “You can work to change it or you can feed into it.”

Willie fed. Eddie worked.

The tough grandmother

Edwards said his decision to live a clean life that would see him serve in the Navy, work as a corrections officer, then have a career that included eight years as one of the state’s highest-ranked police officers, came down to several factors.

“I don’t know what particular thing made me be who I am,” he said.

He loved sports, including football and wrestling, which made him decide to never touch a drop of alcohol. He still hasn’t had a single sip.

His faith in God also kept him from wanting to be as violent as his father and brother.

And even his father shaped him, as Willie Sr., who was illiterate and had a third-grade education, frightened him, but told Eddie he wanted his children to do better than he had.

“He’d say there’s only one dummy in the house, and that’s (Willie Sr.),” Edwards said.

He said he harbors no lingering hostility toward his father, who finally first told Edwards he loved him when Edwards was 32 years old.

“I know that may be silly for some people to believe,” he said. “I can forgive him and not be angry while not accepting what he’s done.

“I never hated my father. But I never admired my father,” he said.
