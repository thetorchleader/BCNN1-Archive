In Cincinnati, Recession-wearied Blacks Stand by Barack Obama

October 20, 2012 Uncategorized

Four years after Barack Obama’s historic triumph, the paradox of black America’s crushing reality flows from Kurt Crews in nine simple words and a shrug that conveys equal parts frustration and forgiveness.

Pictured: Supporters cheer for U.S. President Barack Obama at a campaign rally in Cincinnati last month.
“It’s getting worse — and I’m voting for Barack Obama,” said Crews, 48, a part-time night watchman.
“It took George W. Bush eight years to completely mess things up. It’s going to take eight for Obama to make it right.”
Crews’ views echo throughout Over-The-Rhine, the blighted inner-city Cincinnati neighbourhood that is arguably as good a litmus test as any for African-American political opinion in 2012.
While the U.S. election is heavily freighted with identity politics, from same-sex this to working-women that to the complexities of the surging Hispanic vote, the African-American question remains buried in code.
Some see it in Obama’s rhetoric about haves versus have-nots and the drive for an “even economic playing field”; others in Mitt Romney’s infamous “47 per cent.” Always spoken around, never about.
Take any measure — unemployment, home foreclosure, poverty — and double it: such is the disproportionate price paid by black Americans in the Great Recession. The raw data suggest recession is the wrong word. In this neighbourhood, depression fits better.
Yet overwhelmingly, Obama is expected to retain their support on Nov. 6, polling as much as 95 per cent of the African-American vote — with some surveys suggesting Romney with less than 1 per cent.
Here in Ohio, those numbers may ultimately speak loudest — victory for Obama here could make the difference, giving the Democrats four more years in the White House.
