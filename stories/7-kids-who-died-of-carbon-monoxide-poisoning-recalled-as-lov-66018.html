7 Kids Who Died of Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Recalled as Loving Reflection of Father

April 8, 2015 National

The late children of Rodney Todd are pictured in this undated photo courtesy former coworker Stephanie Wells. Todd and his children were found dead after accidental carbon monoxide exposure at their house. (Photo: Courtesy Stephanie Wells)

Sarah Hardy recalled the children she adored by name.

Six-year-old Tybreyia, who they called Bree-Bree, was a bashful type, always with her thumb in her mouth.

ZhiHeem, 7, a wild child his family nicknamed Bart, was energetic and always getting into everything. Twelve-year-old Tykeria was a diva who loved to put on makeup and prance around the house.

Older son Cameron was a wannabe ladies man, nicknamed Pun. Tyjuziana, 15, the oldest of the five girls, “told me that she wanted to be a model. We’d practice in her old house, in the kitchen, doing the runway walk,” Hardy said.

Inside the unassuming three-bedroom house on Antioch Avenue, each of Rodney Eric Todd’s seven children had a place to sleep. Two boys shared a bedroom, while the younger four girls shared two queen beds in another. Rodney, 36, let his oldest, a 15-year-old daughter, have the master bedroom to herself, while he slept on the couch, according to Hardy.

Police found each of them dead in their beds late Monday, having succumbed to carbon monoxide poisoning.

“It’s weird, because I’m used to them coming out on the step when they hear my car pull up,” Hardy said, her voice cracking with emotion. “It’s not going to be no one coming out that door no more. He’s not coming out that door. It’s hard, I’m not going to lie to you. It’s hard. We had to watch. It’s like we’re waiting for that door to open. It’s not ever going to open again.”

Hardy, 29, said she’s known Rodney Todd for about seven years and considered him her best friend. When she lost her home to fire in 2013, she said Todd was her support system — “my backbone” — and let her stay with him and the kids.

“Basically, I lost everything. He kept me alive. Because there was a lot of days I wanted to give up. If I didn’t have them, it would have been hard, because I was really depressed. Having them in my life, you can’t beat that. It was priceless,” she said.

Hardy said the kids were joyful, respectful, and loving — a reflection of their father. Their mother, who makes her home in Denton, had been mostly out of the picture for about seven years, and Todd had custody.

“They loved their dad to death,” she said. “You couldn’t separate them. Like, they get mad if you try to go out for adult night, you know, just to have fun. That’s how attached they were to their father. Knowing that they haven’t had their mom in their life, it was difficult, but they stuck by their dad. The older ones helped along the way. They were great kids all the way around.”

Rodney and his family had been staying at another rented house down the street, but moved to this new house because the rent was cheaper, according to Hardy. She would visit them often.

“They were getting to the age that, you know, I was doing their hair, cooking for them. I was there every day with them. (The mother) would just pop in, but that would be every six months. These kids barely got to see her,” Hardy said. “It’s hard. Having five girls, they want their mom.”

“I loved them dearly. I was pregnant with twins, and I lost my kids, so being around them was a cheerer-upper. They kept me motivated. When I was sad, they lifted me up.”

Hardy said she believed that electricity to the home was cut off two days before he went to Lowe’s and bought a gasoline-powered electric generator, to power the clothes washer, among other basic household needs.

She heard about the incident when a friend called to say that police had found seven dead bodies in a house behind the post office. Hardy sped down Route 13 to the house, her hands shaking, and her asthma kicking in.

Hardy said since getting the news, she hasn’t been able to hold down a meal without getting sick. She’d been standing outside the house since 7 a.m. Tuesday. Todd’s truck is still parked in the driveway.

“I can’t leave here, because I’m waiting for (him) to walk out that door,” she said. “And it’s not going to happen. I have to give eight final kisses away soon, and I’m not sure how to do it. I have no clue how to even begin.”

The Todd children attended three schools, including Somerset Intermediate School, Princess Anne Elementary School, and Washington High School. Somerset County school administrators met with Superintendent John Gaddis to develop plans for meeting the needs of the students and staff in the wake of student deaths.

“The Somerset County Public School system is deeply saddened and heartbroken over the recent tragedy in the Princess Anne community,” Gaddis said in a statement. “Our hearts and prayers go out to the Todd family and our community as we struggle with the loss of seven beautiful children. We are committed to providing the services needed and meeting the needs of our schools and community during this very difficult time.”

Schools spokesman Leo Lawson declined to make teachers or administrators available for interviews about what it was like teaching the seven Todd children.

“It’s certainly one of the more powerful impacts on the student body that I’ve ever witnessed,” Lawson said. “Right now we feel the grief and pain is too fresh to go down that road.”
