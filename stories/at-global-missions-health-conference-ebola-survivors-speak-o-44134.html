At Global Missions Health Conference, Ebola Survivors Speak of Suffering, Service, and Faith

November 7, 2014 Health

Dr. Rick Sacra, from left, Dr. Kent Brantly and Dr. Deborah Eisenhut speak to the press at Southeast Christian Church on Friday. Brantly, the first Ebola patient treated in the United States gave a talk at 7 p.m. about his missionary work, battling Ebola in Africa, and how perceptions of how the disease is spread need to change. Nov. 7, 2014
(Photo: C-J)

Physician W. Lance Plyler faced a terrible choice: Kent Brantly and Nancy Writebol were both suffering from Ebola in Liberia, but he had only one course of the experimental medicine ZMapp.

“I went to Kent, and he said, ‘Lance, I’d like you to give the therapy to Nancy,'” Plyler said. “I acquiesced to his request.”

But the next day, Brantly’s fever spiked to 104.7 and he was breathing 30 times a minute, and Plyler said, “I was pretty convinced he had just a couple hours to live.”

Writebol hadn’t yet gotten the first dose, which was warming under her arm. Since he was sicker at that point, Brantly ended up getting the first dose of the three-vial therapy they’d been told not to split between patients. The next two doses went to Writebol, and both received the remainder of the therapy after being flown to the United States — and survived.

Plyler, medical director of disaster response at Samaritan’s Purse, and Brantly, a medical missionary with the organization, recalled the harrowing experience at a press conference during the Global Missions Health Conference at Southeast Christian Church Friday. They were joined by Dr. Rick Sacra, a medical missionary and Ebola survivor Brantly called his mentor. Brantly and his wife, Amber, also spoke to a crowd of thousands in the evening.

Brantly and Sacra said their deep Christian faith got them through.

“I have two young children. We listen to children’s worship music. When I was lying in my bed…I just listened to that music,” said Indianapolis native Brantly, 33. “It was children’s music, but it was Scripture. That truth became enough.”

Sacra, 52, said God gave him a sense of peace and calm during his suffering, and “the Lord’s prayer became very important to me.”

Both men became infected while caring for others.

Brantly, who is married with two children, served as medical director for the Samaritan’s Purse Ebola Consolidated Case Management Center in Monrovia. He moved to Liberia in October 2013 to begin a two-year term working with Samaritan’s Purse, and by March he and his colleagues got word that the disease had spread to the West African country. Soon the numbers of patients mounted.

“Taking care of Ebola patients is challenging to say the least. It’s a horrible, terrible, dignity-stripping disease,” Brantly said. “I wasn’t concerned I was going to catch Ebola. We were frustrated on a daily basis with the limitations of what we could do for patients.”

Amber Brantly, a nurse and missionary, said she was not afraid either. “As long as Kent felt safe coming home to us, then I did,” she said.

Brantly said he woke up feeling sick on July 23. He said he doesn’t think he contracted Ebola in the isolation unit, given all the precautions health care workers take; he believes he picked up the virus in the emergency room.

Shortly after falling ill he received ZMapp, which is made using tobacco plants by Kentucky BioProcessing in Owensboro, contracted by drug maker Mapp Biopharmaceutical of San Diego. He was also given blood from a 14-year-old Ebola survivor he treated.

By then, his wife and children had gone back to the United States. “I wanted to be there (in Liberia),” Amber Brantly said. “It was really hard to know how lonely he was…”

Brantly was flown to Emory University Hospital in Atlanta, where he received supportive care and recovered. He was the first American flown to the United States for Ebola treatment.

“It is a reversal of roles” to be a patient, he said. “I think in the future it will help me be a better physician.”

Sacra contracted the disease later in the summer. International Christian mission organization SIM announced in early September he was being flown to The Nebraska Medical Center in Omaha for treatment. Sacra said he received supportive care, plasma from Brantly, and a different experimental medication from the Canadian firm Tekmira.

Since then, Brantly has donated his own plasma to Dallas nurses Nina Pham and Amber Vinson.

Thomas Geisbert, a professor of microbiology and immunology at the University of Texas Medical Branch working on Ebola therapies, said plasma from Ebola survivors is not a proven remedy and it’s unclear how much of a role it has played in the U.S. Ebola recoveries.
