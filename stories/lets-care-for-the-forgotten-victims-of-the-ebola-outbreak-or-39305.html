Let’s Care for the Forgotten Victims of the Ebola Outbreak – Orphans

October 13, 2014 Health, National

Emmanuel Junior Cooper of Monrovia, Liberia, lost his parents to Ebola.
(Photo: Jerome Delay, AP)

Many children, after their parents die from the disease, are feared and ostracized.

President Obama’s decision last month to send 3,000 U.S. troops to West Africa to help contain the Ebola epidemic that has killed more than 4,000 people is perhaps the first real effort by the international community to seriously address the outbreak after months of inaction. Although attention in this country is currently focused on a Dallas hospital nurse who became infected after helping care for a Liberian patient who died from Ebola last week, it is important to stay focused on the devastation it is causing in West Africa

Ebola kills about half of those it infects. Transmitted through bodily fluid, and therefore less infective than airborne pathogens such as measles or the flu, it has nonetheless resulted in widespread social upheaval and panic in Liberia and Sierra Leone as people scramble to protect theselves and their families.

One of the terrible consequences of this epidemic has been the high number of children who, after having been orphaned from their parents, have been ostracized because of fear that they may transmit the disease to whomever takes them in. The West and Central African regional director for UNICEF, Manuel Fontaine, recently estimated the number of children orphaned by Ebola as of Sept. 30 to be at least 3,700. A recent report by the BBC described children being “discovered alone in the hospitals where their parents had died or in their communities (and) where, if they were lucky, they were being fed by neighbors.”

With the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention projecting that the number of people infected by Ebola in West Africa could reach 1.4 million by January 2015, the wide-scale orphaning, stigmatization and abandonment of hundreds of thousands of children could be disastrous and have far-reaching repercussions.

Disease-related stigma can be extremely powerful, and often leads to the marginalization and victimization of those to whom it attaches. Because this stigma emerges from underlying cultural attitudes — as opposed to arising spontaneously within individuals — it is pervasive and very difficult to avoid. In this country, for example, the stigma attached to obesity results in many overweight women failing to take full advantage of preventive medicine such as advance cancer screening. These women, in turn, are at higher risk of being diagnosed with more advanced disease with a less-favorable prognosis.

Whereas the stigma associated with obesity derives from social conventions about beauty and desirability, that of infectious disease arises from existential fear and is therefore often much stronger and harsher. In many parts of the developing world — including Africa — patients with AIDS are treated as social outcasts, shunned rather than embraced and cared for as is the case in Western countries where anti-viral medications are widely available.
