At Dallas Festival of Ideas, T. D. Jakes Says City’s Problems Can Be Solved With Three Ingredients: a Cause, a Cure, and a Call

March 2, 2015 Christian

Nathan Hunsinger/Staff Photographer
T.D. Jakes gives the closing remarks during The Dallas Festival of Ideas.

The first new idea at the Dallas Festival of Ideas? How to hold the event after an ice storm.

A planned night and full day of speeches and conversations were compressed into one afternoon Saturday as more than a dozen speakers and upward of 700 attendees managed weather challenges to attend the event. They addressed some of the most intractable issues facing Dallas.

“Too much work, too many smart people, too many important ideas to be addressed for us to let a little slush get in the way,” Dallas Mayor Mike Rawlings said shortly before the conference started.

The first two hours, which included national speakers and several local artists, produced an impressive potential agenda of actionable ideas, some more ambitious than others.

From author Luis Alberto Urrea: Why not create a Dallas book festival?

Urrea held up Tucson as a city that got into the book fest business only recently, but has turned it into one of the nation’s biggest and best.

The payoff, he said, was cultural and financial.

He pointed out that Dallas has the George W. Bush Presidential Center at Southern Methodist University. Former first lady Laura Bush remains popular, and literacy has long been her big issue, he said.

“I’m calling out to whoever might set this up: Make her the honorary chairman,” Urrea said. “Who can deny her?”

Ta-Nehisi Coates, senior editor and writer for The Atlantic, took on race and politics. Dallas officials and citizens should “work harder to take the force of racism in our society more seriously,” he said.

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 was supposed to allow federal authorities to reduce segregation, but it hasn’t been used effectively, Coates said.

“Cities don’t have to wait for [the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development] to do its job,” he said.

Dallas could create a squad of mystery shoppers to see whether landlords or home sellers are discriminating against blacks, he said.

Rahaf Harfoush, who calls herself a “digital innovation and foresight strategist,” tossed out a Google-search worth of suggestions for how Dallas could better use the Internet. Some of her ideas: Build physical infrastructure to bring broadband to more people; ask the city to create an app that shows where crime is happening; have the city build an app where people can register and receive tailored alerts about things that matter to them; hire a chief digital officer for Dallas, as has been done in several places.

Elizabeth Green, author of Building a Better Teacher: How Teaching Works (and How to Teach It to Everyone), suggested that Dallas should boost training resources for teachers.

In most school districts, she said, teaching is considered almost private, leaving too many teachers isolated and without much help from peers or experts.

“We treat it like Victorian-era sex,” she said.

Vishaan Chakrabarti, real estate professor at Columbia University and a partner at New York, N.Y.-based SHoP Architects, suggested wholesale changes in the way downtown Dallas should grow. The 20th-century model of separating work and home, leading to residential suburbs far from jobs, should be history, he said.

Instead, Dallas planners should think about “nodes,” with work, education, government and other services built near where people live. Dallas has great assets for growth, he said: lots of undeveloped land, relatively low taxes and access to a great airport.

Breakout panels tackled culture, physical environs, politics, education and innovation.
