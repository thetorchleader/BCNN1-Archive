Rev. Mark Tyler, Pastor of Mother Bethel AME Church in Philly, Leads Effort to Reclaim Historic Burial Ground Site

March 29, 2015 Black

Rev. Mark Tyler, pastor of Mother Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church, is at the forefront of an effort to reclaim a historic burial ground near the heart of the city. — Photo: Abdul S. Sulayman, Tribune Chief Photographer

The Bethel Burial Grounds remains a contested historical site near the heart of the city.

For over a century children have been playing on land that was intended to be sacred. It is land where some of the city’s most prominent African Americans are buried — men and women who historically are regarded as the founders of the Black community in Philadelphia. Sarah Bass Allen, Stephen Laws, the Rev. John Boggs and James Champion, an original member of Mother Bethel — a historic church founded by Richard Allen in 1794 — lie in its soil.

Ignatius Beck, a freed slave whose story is similar to that of Solomon Northrup, recently immortalized in the film “Twelve Years a Slave,” was a member of Mother Bethel and is among those buried there. Beck was re-enslaved on a trip to Virginia and was in forced servitude for two years before he managed to escape bondage and return to Philadelphia.

The Bethel Burying Ground is beneath part of the Weccacoe Playground at 4th and Queen streets in South Philadelphia. It lies beneath the southern end of the playground. Of importance is the preservation of the burial ground as a protected historical site, which has yet to happen. But it is an issue which is moving in the right direction, according to the Rev. Mark Tyler, pastor of historic Mother Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church.

“Right now we’re waiting to hear from the mayor regarding commemoration of the site,” Tyler said. “Everyone who has expressed a concern about the site — different community groups, archeologists, city officials — all agree on its importance and the need to work out a plan regarding its preservation and commemoration. The community building and the small bathroom inside it sits squarely on the burial site and we’re waiting to hear from the city.”

Mark McDonald, a spokesman for the Nutter administration said things are moving forward.

“At the moment there’s nothing new to report but I can say things are moving and in a good direction,” McDonald said. “However I can’t talk about it yet.”

The history of the site goes back to Allen, founder of Mother Bethel church, located at 6th and Lombard streets. Allen purchased the land for the cemetery in 1810. It was sold to the city in 1889, when the church fell into financial difficulty, which is where the modern contention begins. Local historian Terry Buckalew uncovered the site eight years ago when working on a documentary about Octavius Catto.
