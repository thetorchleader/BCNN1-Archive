Why the Rising Suicide Rate Among Young Black Children Is a Reason For All People to Come Together and Do Something About It

May 26, 2015 Black

Mark Norwine, a bullying prevention coordinator with CHADS Coaltion on Mental Health, talks to Ferguson Middle School seventh graders about identifying signs of depression and suicide during a health class presentation on Oct. 4, 2012. Photo by Robert Cohen, rcohen@post-dispatch.com

A new study showing that the suicide rate among black children ages 5 to 11 has nearly doubled since the early 1990s, while the rate for white children has declined, should be heard as a “call to action.”

Those are the words of Sean Joe, a professor of social work at Washington University who studies suicide among black youth. Mr. Joe did not participate in the new study, done by experts at the Research Institute at Nationwide Children’s Hospital in Columbus, Ohio. The study was published last week in the journal JAMA Pediatrics.

Mr. Joe said the climbing rate of suicides among African-American children is “alarming, disturbing … shocking. What are we supposed to think when these very young children don’t think life is worth living?”

Youth suicide is relatively rare but still is the second-leading cause of death among adolescents in the United States. It is the 11th-leading cause of death among pre-adolescent children ages 5 to 11.

Because it is so rare in that age group — 657 children between those ages died by suicide from 1993 to 2012 — there is not much data to work with. Researchers in this study had to blend several years of data to get reliable results.

Suicide deaths among black children increased from 1.35 per million to 2.54 per million within the time frame that was studied. Suicide deaths among white children decreased from 1.14 per million to 0.77 per million.

Most of the victims were boys, 84 percent or 553 children, compared with 104 girls. While guns are used by slightly more than half the adolescents and young adults who commit suicide, hanging and suffocation was the leading method of suicide among the elementary-school-aged children.

This was the first national study that found a higher suicide rate for blacks than for whites in any age group, the researchers noted. They offered a few possible explanations for the difference, including greater exposure to violence and traumatic stress among African-American children and earlier onset of puberty, which can increase depression, impulsiveness and aggression.

Mr. Joe suggested another reason: the breakdown in black communities of traditional stabilizing social forces, such as churches. He said young people in the communities are not as religiously observant as their elders, so they don’t experience the same sort of protection from the church that made older African-Americans less vulnerable to suicide.
