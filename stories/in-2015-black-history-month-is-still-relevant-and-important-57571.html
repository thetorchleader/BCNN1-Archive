In 2015, Black History Month Is Still Relevant and Important for Young People to Study

February 19, 2015 Black

Audience members at the Unity of Faith Missionary Baptist Church turn to watch as the Unity of Faith Steppers enter the sanctuary and begin their inspirational step routine during the Black History Program on Saturday afternoon.

Were the contributions of African-Americans highlighted in school books or featured on the nightly news 50 years ago? People of color who lived through those troubled times say no — absolutely not — to that question.

For years, the role that blacks, Hispanics, Native Americans and Asian-Americans played in shaping American society was left out of history books.

To address what he saw as a problem, historian Carter G. Woodson campaigned for what was known in 1926 as Negro History Week. Later, Negro History Week became Black History Month.

In communities big and small all across the country, Black History Month is still meaningful today. School children and adults now celebrate past contributions made by African-Americans.

“It’s a time for the nation to recognize the major milestones and key figures in African-American history,” Huntsville truck driver Ennis Wilson said. “This country is headed up by the first president of Negro descent. Blacks are widely represented in the government, books, TV, athletics and everywhere.”

The need for Black History Month has been called into question by some younger blacks. Given the local celebrations, ReSean Louis, 20, asks if it is still necessary to set aside a month for the express purpose of celebrating the accomplishments of African-Americans.
