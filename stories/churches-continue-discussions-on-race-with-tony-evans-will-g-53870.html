Churches Continue Discussions On Race With Tony Evans, Will Graham, and Others

January 27, 2015 Black, Christian

KB Frazier, (right) from Central Reform Congregation, leads the opening prayer with song and his drum on Sunday afternoon, Jan. 25, 2015, at St. Louis University, at the start of a gathering of area religious leaders who met to have conversations on race, racism and prejudice. At left is David Gerth, Executive Director of Metropolitan Congregations United. Photo by J.B. Forbes, jforbes@post-dispatch.com

Since the death of African-American teenager Michael Brown, civil rights activists have called for change in police departments and municipal courts — but also in the way society thinks about black lives.

Not content to sit back, a wide array of churches are leading the charge, intentionally prolonging the conversation on race that has left some in the region uncomfortable.

“God is engineering liberation,” said Dietra Wise Baker, pastor at Liberation Christian Church and co-chair of Metropolitan Congregations United, a group of interdenominational, multiracial congregations from around the region.

It “can’t be a people that rise up, but the people. We have to rise up together. This can’t be one community’s fight.”

Baker was speaking at a gathering Sunday of more than 300 faithful at St. Louis University for an event dubbed Sacred Conversations on Race (+Action). The event marked the first of many conversations on race sponsored by the group in the coming months.

Around the region, houses of worship are spearheading sometimes uncomfortable talks on inequality, white privilege and the message behind the often-heard chant “black lives matter,” in the hope that society can come together and collectively work for change.

In a separate Sunday gathering across town at Faith Church St. Louis in Earth City, for example, about 1,000 people met with faith leaders such as William Franklin Graham IV, grandson of evangelical preacher Billy Graham, and Aeneas Williams, a former St. Louis Ram turned pastor.

Their pastors signed the “Ferguson Declaration,” a commitment “to support our leaders, our neighbors, and each other as we rise from the ashes of Ferguson and become an even better St. Louis.”

Faith leaders say “rising from the ashes” necessitates acknowledging past mistakes.

“I think that God wants us to change. God never has to change, but we do,” Stoney Shaw, senior pastor of First Baptist Church of Ferguson, said recently, when speaking about the declaration. “I think he uses the affairs of men. … Everybody was bopping along until the tragedy, Michael Brown.”

“God gave an opportunity for change.”

Or as the declaration reads, referring to 2 Chronicles, a book of the Bible, we humbly come together before God “seeking his face, turning from our wicked ways, crying out to the Lord to forgive our sins … and to heal our land.”

“Faith without works is dead,” the declaration reads, “and we dedicate ourselves to pray together that we may discover God’s plans for us to equip and engage his people in practical and united ways of serving our communities.”

Keynote speaker Tony Evans, a nationally known preacher, urged the many hundreds at Faith Church St. Louis to become “a rock of unity, a rock of oneness,” yet welcomed diversity.

“God intended differences,” Evans said. But “he did not mean differences to create injustice.” He called on everyone here to “demonstrate you can take a mess and make a miracle.”

Williams urged those attending not to leave the next generation “the baton of injustice,” but rather “the love of Dr. Martin Luther King,” judging people not by the color of their skin but by the content of their character.

The event was hosted and organized by Vivian Dudley, founder of One Church Outreach Ministry.

The Dred Scott Heritage Foundation, an organization based in Florissant that aims to educate the public about the 19th-century African-American slave who sued for his freedom, helped promote the meeting.
