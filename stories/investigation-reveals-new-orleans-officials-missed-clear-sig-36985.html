Investigation Reveals New Orleans Officials Missed Clear Signs of Canal’s Collapse

September 4, 2014 National

Wayne Weiser retired as a Corps of Engineers supervisor in 2000 after nearly 40 years. Bothered by the perception that Corps design work caused the 17th Street Canal levee failure, he dug for and found records that show local maintenance issues at play.
(Photo: WWL-TV, New Orleans)

Nine years after Hurricane Katrina’s surge caused the 17th Street Canal floodwall to collapse, a WWL-TV investigation has uncovered documents that strongly suggest a dredging project compromised levee stability and authorities missed the clear signs.

The records show that local and federal authorities could have recognized the damaging effects of the work if only they had followed specific instructions to monitor them.

The TV station also found that some of the same concerns first raised by a Corps of Engineers official in the 1980s have become an issue again after Katrina, this time on the Jefferson Parish side of the canal. Corps officials insist that they are on top of it.

On Aug. 29, 2005, a two-block stretch of the 17th Street Canal floodwall collapsed just south of the Hammond Highway bridge, inundating the Lakeview section of New Orleans. Although much shorter than the one on the New Orleans side, the wall on the Jefferson Parish side of the canal remained erect.

Investigators from the Corps, Congress, the American Society of Civil Engineers, the state of Louisiana and the University of California at Berkeley all questioned why. They concluded that engineering errors in the design of the floodwalls were the primary cause, with high water levels causing a gap to form between walls and supporting metal sheet piles.

Some of the reports also noted concerns with dredging of the canal bottom, a project started by the New Orleans Sewerage and Water Board in 1990. But they said there was not enough information available to truly assess how dredging in the canal had affected the levee’s stability.

No one, including the local levee districts, the Corps and their engineering contractors, have been able to locate underwater surveys of the canal bottom, not even any post-construction documents that should have been provided to prove the work was done properly.

“At this time, none of the studies nor the available documentation has been able to identify the actual dredged profile of the canal after the dredging operations were completed nor during the intervening years,” said Bob Bea, the Cal-Berkeley professor who led the independent study team, in a court declaration.

That is, until Wayne Weiser, a retired Corps engineer who spent 20 years overseeing levees and waterways, stumbled upon cross-sections of the dredged Orleans side of the canal.

Buried in an East Jefferson Levee District contract from 1992, the drawings show that the canal bank all along the area that would collapse during Katrina was concave, dipping far beyond the straight-edge slope depicted in the dredging plans. Weiser said that compromised the strength of the wedge that was supposed to support the floodwall, and suggested that more erosion could easily have occurred over the next decade before the storm’s high-pressure waters pushed the wall down.

What’s more, Weiser found a 1988 letter by the district’s chief engineer showing that erosion was a concern going into the dredging project.
