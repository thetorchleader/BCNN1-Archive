Historic Black Church in West Baden, Indiana Is Endangered

May 6, 2014 African-American

The Gothic Revival-style West Baden Colored Church is up for sale for $1 to anyone who will restore it.
(Photo: Jody Miller)

The century-old Baptist church, once the spiritual home to blacks who migrated north for jobs at the fancy Springs Valley hotels, sits abandoned and crumbling.

Rickety plywood stairs descend into 6 inches of standing water in the basement beneath the First Baptist Church, which became known as the West Baden Colored Church. The ceiling in the sanctuary reveals huge gaps into the attic. The east wall is extremely bowed.

In 1909, West Baden Springs Hotel owner Lee W. Sinclair deeded the site to the church founders, many of whom worked at his hotel. The Rev. C.R. Parrish served as the first preacher.

Last month, the Indiana Landmarks group placed the church on the state’s 10 Most Endangered Buildings list, hoping the designation will call attention to the last remaining African-American landmark in the Springs Valley.

The church congregation dwindled over the years, and the church’s final member, Dorothy Smith, gave the church and property to the local historical society in 1992. In return, the society agreed to continue mowing the cemetery where most of the town’s black citizens are buried.

Longtime historical society member and preservationist Jeff Lane said the organization tried to stabilize the church around 1990. Building and trades students from Springs Valley High School tore out the old walls and hung new drywall throughout the slant-floored structure. They put on a new roof. Historical society members “adopted” the stained-glass windows, paying about $250 each for the geometric three-colored windows to be repaired.

They salvaged the pews and brought two majestic altar chairs up from the basement. The old, heavy walnut pulpit is still there, as is the enamel tub used for baptisms. There’s an organ and a piano, covered with cobwebs and dust. The church bell still tolls, with a tug on a thick rope.

“This place just has too much potential to let it vanish like everything else related to the black heritage of the town has,” Lane said.
