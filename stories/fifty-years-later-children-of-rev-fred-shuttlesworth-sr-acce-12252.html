Fifty Years Later, Children of Rev. Fred Shuttlesworth Sr., Accept the International Freedom Conductor Award for Their Father’s Work during the Civil Rights Movement in Birmingham

May 10, 2013 Uncategorized

Fifty years ago on May 2 and May 3, Birmingham leaders used fire hoses, police dogs and jailings to stop waves of students who marched out of a church and on to downtown streets seeking equal rights for Blacks in the culmination of a civil rights movement in that city started by Birmingham pastor Rev. Fred Shuttlesworth Sr., who later lived and founded a church in Cincinnati.


Civil Rights leader Rev. Fred Shuttlesworth Sr., of Birmingham and Cincinnati was awarded the International Freedom Conductor Award by the National Underground Railroad Freedom Center earlier this year in a ceremony at the Freedom Center in Cincinnati. His children, from left, Fred Shuttlesworth Jr., Carolyn Shuttlesworth, Ruby Shuttlesworth Bester and Patricia Shuttlesworth Massengil, attended the ceremony to accept the award for their father posthumously. At right is Judge Nathaniel Jones of Cincinnati, who presented the award. In honor of Rev Fred Shuttlesworth and his International Freedom Conductor Award, the Freedom Center donated $25,000 to Greater New Light Baptist Church, which he founded in North Avondale.
The Birmingham Campaign was organized by the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights, co-founded and led by the late Rev.Shuttlesworth, who founded the organization a week after the State of Alabama banned the NAACP in the state, and the Atlanta-based Southern Christian Leadership Conference, led by the Rev. Martin Luther King Jr. Rights. Rev. Shuttlesworth Sr. moved to Cincinnati in 1961 to take up the pastorage of the Revelation Baptist Church. However, he remained intensely involved in the Birmingham struggle after moving to Cincinnati.
Although in Cincinnati in 1963, he courageously returned to Birmingham every Monday to attend meetings of the Alabama Christian Movement for Human At the urging of his wife, he had moved his family to Cincinnati to protect them and so they could further their education. The Shuttlesworth home in Birmingham had been bombed Christmas night of 1956 and his church was also bombed. There were no injuries in the bombings.
He was apparently personally fearless, even though he was aware of the risks he ran. Other committed activists were scared off or mystified by his willingness to accept the risk of death. Rev. Shuttlesworth himself vowed to “kill segregation or be killed by it.”
He died in Birmingham in October 2011 at the age of 89. He had lived in Cincinnati for 40 years after leaving Birmingham in the 1960s.
The Birmingham Campaign, or the so-called “Children’s Crusade,” was organized by the Alabama Christian Movement for Human Rights and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC) to bring attention to the unequal treatment that Black Americans endured in Birmingham. Led by Reverends Freed Shuttlesworth, Martin Luther King Jr., and Ralph Abernathy and Fred Shuttlesworth Sr., and others, the spring 1963 campaign of nonviolent direct actions culminated in widely publicized confrontations between Black youth and White civic authorities, and eventually led the municipal government to change the city’s discrimination laws. More than 2,500 people were arrested over the two days.
Ruby Shuttlesworth Bester of Cincinnati, said her father, who was in Birmingham to organize the demonstrations, was hit by a fire hose during confrontations with police and rushed to a hospital with injuries. Public Safety Director Eugene “Bull” Conner said while an ambulance transported Rev. Shuttlesworth to the hospital that he wished he was being taken away in a hearse instead of an ambulance, she said.
In the early 1960s, Birmingham was one of the most racially divided cities in the United States. Black citizens faced legal and economic disparities, and violent retribution when they attempted to bring attention to their problems. Protests in Birmingham began with a boycott meant to pressure business leaders to open employment to people of all races and end segregation in public facilities, restaurants, and stores. When business leaders resisted the boycott, SCLC organizer Wyatt Tee Walker and Birmingham native Fred Shuttlesworth began what they called Project C (for confrontation), a series of sit-ins and marches intended to provoke mass arrests. After the campaign ran low on adult volunteers, high school, college, and elementary students were trained by SCLC coordinator James Bevel to participate, resulting in hundreds of arrests. To dissuade demonstrators and control the protests, the Birmingham Police Department, led by Eugene “Bull” Connor, used high-pressure water jets and police dogs on children and bystanders. Not all of the demonstrators were peaceful, despite the avowed intentions of the SCLC. In some cases, bystanders attacked the police, who responded with force. King and the SCLC drew some criticism for putting children in harm’s way.
