Robert Singleton, Who Fought Segregation as a Freedom Rider, to Be Honored by UCLA

February 15, 2015 Black

Robert Singleton was an African-American soldier in basic training in Augusta, Georgia, when he was ordered by a white driver to go to the back of a nearly empty public bus.

“I was being trained to protect that driver, and he didn’t even want me on his bus,” recalled Singleton, who later went on to earn an M.A. in 1962 and Ph.D. in 1983 from UCLA. “I felt like I was not a citizen of the U.S. … like I was serving a country that didn’t value me.”
After military service in Europe, Singleton set out to serve his country in a different way — by fighting injustice at home — when he led his wife, Helen Singleton, and eight other UCLA students, including Robert Farrell, on a history-making journey to join some 400 civil rights activists on what was called the Freedom Rides to challenge segregation in interstate travel in 1961.
Robert Singleton was arrested in 1961 as a Freedom Rider

The Singletons and Farrell, who graduated from UCLA with a B.A. in 1961, are among those who returned to campus Feb. 6 for “Journey to Change: From the Freedom Riders to Ferguson, Missouri,” a celebration and panel discussion in Royce Hall. UCLA law professor Cheryl Harris, chair of the Department of African-American Studies moderated the event. Chancellor Gene Block delivered welcome remarks and UCLA Student Affairs, working with Alumni Affairs, Undergraduate Students Association Council and other student groups, and the Los Angeles Urban League organized the event.

“Journey to Change” grew out of discussions between the Singletons and leaders of the Afrikan Student Union and USAC. When students brought up the idea of honoring the Freedom Riders, Vice Chancellor for Student Affairs Janina Montero said she saw the importance of highlighting UCLA students’ role in the important historical chapter.  More than a history lesson, however, the event has evolved into an opportunity to reflect both on past activism and on the lessons it offers today.

“While we were working on the event, the grand jury decisions in the Michael Brown and Eric Garner cases came down,” said Denise Pacheco, student affairs executive officer who helped organize the panel. “One of our goals became asking, ‘What advice would you give community leaders working on these issues today?’ We don’t want to just honor the Freedom Riders; we want to hear from them.” There’s one piece of advice Singleton said he would offer:  “Have the emotional discipline to work together.” Helen Singleton added:  “Do your research; understand the right tactics and strategies to use.”
