Vice President Biden Delivers Keynote Address at NAACP Dinner in Detroit; National Racial Unrest is Main Topic in Speeches

May 4, 2015 Black

Attendees clap as Vice President Joe Biden speaks at the 60th NAACP Fight for Freedom Fund Dinner on Sunday, May 3,2015 at the COBO Conference Center in Detroit. (PHOTO CREDIT: Kirthmon F. Dozier, Detroit Free Press)

An old African tribal expression should become the rallying cry for a nation roiled by racial unrest, said Vice President Joe Biden tonight in Detroit.

” ‘We see you. We see you.’ That should be the guiding principle for rebuilding trust between police and African Americans,” he told a crowd of nearly 10,000 people attending the 60th annual NAACP Fight for Freedom Fund dinner.

“You can’t expect relationships between the police and community to improve unless there are actual relationships,” Biden said. “Trying to bridge the gap between community and law enforcement is pretty basic.”

The shadow of shootings of black men by police officers in Ferguson, Mo., Charleston, S.C., and Baltimore, Md., hung heavy over the dinner as speakers talked of the incidents and the civil unrest that followed.

But the Rev. Wendell Anthony, president of the Detroit branch of the NAACP, said it’s up to the leadership of the business community, city and the NAACP itself to provide the examples to ensure that Detroit doesn’t become the next riot-torn urban community.

“We went through training to show young African Americans what not to do when you encounter police. That turns my stomach to have to do that. But we’re forced to do that because the reality is, they may not come home alive otherwise,” he said. “But what we’re doing at the Freedom Fund dinner is providing all these examples of people who are doing great work and others can take that back to the community.”

Biden applauded the era of community policing in the late 1990s when 100,000 additional police officers were dispatched to the nation’s cities. It was a time when the police got out of their cars and got to know the business owners and residents of neighborhoods.

“Violent crime fell in those early years … And then we stopped funding it,” Biden said. “And now there are too many mothers in my hometown and in Detroit and inner cities who worry every time they open their door and see their son walk out the door. … They worry that some authority will see their son as a profile, not as an individual.”

The dinner was founded to raise money to fight the racial inequities and violence of the times. It has grown from the 300 people who attended the first dinner to hear Thurgood Marshall, who had just argued the Brown versus Board of Education case and would later become a U.S. Supreme Court Justice, to the 10,000 people who regularly attend the dinner to hear presidents, vice presidents, civil rights leaders and influential ministers.

It’s the biggest fund raiser for the organization and was expected to raise close to $500,000 this year.
