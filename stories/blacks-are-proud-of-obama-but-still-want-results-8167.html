Blacks are Proud of Obama, but Still Want Results

September 7, 2012 Uncategorized

African-Americans still take pride in President Barack Obama’s election — but four years later, they’re also still looking for results.

Pictured: Critics say President Obama has distanced himself from black issues. | AP Photo
Recalling the hopes and dreams of 2008, they’re left wondering what happened to the Obama who stood for helping the poor, bringing down inner-city violence and advocating for them. There are complaints that he didn’t invite every black leader to every event they wanted to be at, attend every black group’s convention, or prioritize every concern brought to him by the Congressional Black Caucus.
In office, Obama’s interactions with the African-American community have largely taken two forms: controversies — including just last month, when he was forced to defend Vice President Joe Biden’s “chains” comment — and paying tribute to the civil rights movement and its leaders.
He hasn’t directly said much more, aside from his contentious response that police acted “stupidly” during Henry Louis Gates’s 2009 arrest or the comments he finally delivered in March about Trayvon Martin’s killing — “If I had a son, he’d look like Trayvon,” he said, in what people close to him described as more an offering of empathy than a racial commentary.
The overwhelming majority of African-Americans appear willing to cut him some slack — especially when presented with an election-year choice — but there’s a long list of prominent critics who say that Obama has cruelly distanced himself from them in the interest of appealing to white America. With the work they did to elect Obama, they’re enraged to see the man in the Oval Office say, as he did in August to Black Enterprise magazine, “I’m not the president of black America. I’m the president of the United States of America.”
“I think I’ll vomit,” said Cornel West, the religion and African-American studies scholar and activist who retired from Princeton University earlier this year, referring to times when Obama distances himself from the African-American community. “It’s nothing but a ploy.”
Despite the disappointments about Obama’s agenda, there’s still a widespread feeling that Obama’s election and his presidency really have had an impact on the black community.
Hilary Shelton, director of the NAACP’s Washington bureau, said Obama has shown that “even those with a line of slavery could actually get elected to the White House.” The result, he said, is “an increase in college applications from black students who think, ‘Maybe I can achieve, maybe this isn’t a ruse, maybe I too can achieve the presidency.'”
Actor Hill Harper, who met Obama when they were both students at Harvard Law School, said the at-risk black students he works with through his foundation have high aspirations. Though they’d be the first in their families to go to college, they look to Obama and Harper and say, “Well, I’m definitely going to go to grad school.”
With most of those kids being raised by single mothers, they look to the Obamas as an example of the kind of family they’d like to have, with two parents raising kids, Harper said.
