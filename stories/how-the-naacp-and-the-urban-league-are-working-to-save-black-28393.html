How the NAACP and the Urban League Are Working to Save Black Communities

Date not recorded in the original archive Black

DAVID ZALAZNIK/JOURNAL STAR | Tri-County Urban League CEO Laraine Bryson stands in the organization’s conference room where a portrait of League’s first executive director, Frank Campbell, hangs on the wall in the background.

The late Frank Campbell specialized in labor and industrial relations.

But in the late 1980s and early 1990s, he was preaching, sometimes in three or four speeches a month, about the unprecedented opportunities for minorities on the horizon, particularly for African-Americans, Peoria’s largest racial or ethnic minority group.

White men would account for only 15 percent of the new labor force by 2000, Campbell, executive director of the Tri-County Urban League, warned. White women, African-Americans, Latinos and Asian-Americans would make up the other 85 percent. Employers, facing work force shortages, would be forced to hire groups they had traditionally ignored.

Labor shortages had opened opportunities for minorities during World War Il, Campbell said, and changing demographics and a changing economy was about to prompt the remix in the next century.

Young black people, Campbell told employers, teachers, preachers, young black people, and anyone else within earshot, were on the verge of becoming the most successful generation of black people in the country’s history. But they have to be prepared.

“Most economists are saying things are going to be a hell of a lot worse for black people, and they’re probably right,” Campbell said almost 25 years ago. “But I’m trying to encourage incentive, not kill it.”

Same old problems

As the Urban League in Peoria approaches its 50th anniversary next year, Campbell’s successor, Laraine Bryson, contemplates the social, political and economic cycles that have shaped the agency’s mission.

The Urban League always has had a single, simple mission — to expand opportunities for those disadvantaged in some way, she says. But the route the agency takes may change, depending on political and economic winds of the moment.

As the local chapter of the NAACP approaches its 100th anniversary in 2015, its president is as strident as ever, agitating for opportunities for African-Americans.
