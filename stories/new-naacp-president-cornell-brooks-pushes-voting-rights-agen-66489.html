New NAACP President Cornell Brooks Pushes Voting Rights Agenda

April 10, 2015 Black

NAACP president Cornell Brooks speaks at Cabrini College. He backed grassroots efforts to get African Americans to the polls and combating social injustices. (ERICA ABBOTT)

Cornell Brooks is small of stature and slight of build, but there was nothing slight about the NAACP president’s voice once he began addressing the crowd.

His fists shaking and his voice bellowing, he delivered his speech before the Cabrini College audience as though it were a Sunday sermon.

“Fifty years,” he roared, referencing the recent anniversary of the historic march in Selma, Ala., that helped lead to the 1965 Voting Rights Act.

And yet, he thundered, “for every act of justice, there seems to be an equal and opposite action of injustice.”

Brooks spoke to about 200 people at Cabrini, where he was an “executive in residence” for the day Thursday, part of the Radnor college’s unity week.

In July, Brooks assumed leadership of the nation’s most prominent civil rights organization, which has been the subject of questions, even by former leaders, about whether it still can fight effectively for equal rights.

It can, Brooks said after his talk.

Brooks inherited a host of internal and external problems as the NAACP’s 18th leader. Months before he took the helm, the NAACP laid off 7 percent of its workers amid budget concerns.

Brooks said the organization has built numerous grassroots programs, including “Souls to the Polls,” a campaign aimed at getting congregants of black churches to vote early in elections after church services. This summer, Brooks said, the NAACP is planning an 850-mile march from Selma to Washington to advocate for voting rights.

“When I took over, people always asked, ‘Is the NAACP relevant?’ ” Brooks said. “Nobody asks that question anymore.”
