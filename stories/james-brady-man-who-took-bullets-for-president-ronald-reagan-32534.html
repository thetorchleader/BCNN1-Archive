James Brady, Man Who Took Bullets for President Ronald Reagan, Dies at 73; Remembered for Honesty and Credibility

August 5, 2014 National

James Brady

James Brady will be remembered by history as a brave and unlucky man whose brain was forever damaged by gunshots in a failed attempt on President Ronald Reagan’s life on March 30, 1981.

Brady struggled throughout the rest of his life, which ended yesterday at age 73. The right side of his brain was damaged, affecting his speech and short-term memory. With his left arm paralyzed and his left leg weakened, it was a struggle for him to walk even a few steps.

Brady knew what had happened to him. Instead of dwelling on it, he and his determined wife, Sarah, became apostles of background checks for gun owners, with emphasis on the mentally ill. John W. Hinckley, the man who shot Brady and Reagan after first planning to shoot his predecessor Jimmy Carter, was mentally troubled throughout his life but never had difficulty acquiring firearms. The bill that Congress eventually passed, with support from Nancy Reagan, was known as the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act. It was signed into law by President Clinton on Nov. 30, 1993.

After the murder of schoolchildren at Newtown, Conn., in 2012, the Bradys urged new restrictions on firearms, none of which have found favor with Congress. Those of us fortunate to know Brady before a portion of his brain was shot away remember him as a witty, fun-loving and outspoken man who greatly enlivened the Reagan plane during the 1980 campaign.

He was, in fact, so outspoken that it’s remarkable that he ever became White House press secretary in the first place. In fact, he almost didn’t last through the campaign after an episode that landed Brady in hot water with the campaign’s top brass.

The background was a Reagan flight of fancy during a speech in Steubenville, Ohio, to an audience of steelworkers and coal miners who understandably were more concerned about preserving their threatened jobs than the environment. Tired after a long day of campaigning, Reagan ventured the “suspicion” that the volcanic eruption of Mount St. Helens had “released more sulfur dioxide into the atmosphere of the world than has been released in the last 10 years of automobile driving.” He was off a bit: The count at the time was 2,000 tons of sulfur dioxide a day for Mount St. Helens and 81,000 tons for automobiles.

Reagan made this declaration on Oct. 7, 1980, and his Democratic adversaries seized on it, tying it to an earlier Reagan suggestion that trees and other vegetation are responsible for much of the nation’s air pollution. When Reagan spoke in Claremont, Calif., five days later, someone hoisted a picture of a tree with the caption “Chop Me Down Before I Kill Again.”

The press corps thought this was funny. So did the Reagan staff — and although we didn’t know it at the time, so did Reagan. On one campaign flight, speechwriter Ken Khachigian noticed a forest fire below, and softly said, “Killer trees.” Brady laughed and repeated the statement in his usually loud voice. The next day Brady was quoted saying “killer trees” in the New York Times and William Casey, the official campaign manager at the Reagan headquarters in Arlington, Va., ordered him removed from the campaign plane.

Stuart K. Spencer, the political consultant and a decisive presence in the campaign, waited a few days and with the help of Reagan’s trusted aide Michael K. Deaver, quietly brought Brady back on LeaderShip ’80, the campaign plane. It was not a sentimental decision. Both Spencer and Deaver explained to me that Brady was good to have around because his wisecracks relieved the tension of a long campaign — and also because the press trusted him.

We had reason to do so. Brady was one of the few people I have ever known in politics who didn’t try to spin reporters. If Reagan made a slip, Brady was the first to acknowledge it. His honesty gave Brady credibility when he was advancing the Reagan cause.

James A. Baker III, the talented Texan whom Reagan named as his White House chief of staff, sounded out the press corps on matters in which they had an interest. After the election he asked several reporters, including me, who we wanted as the White House press secretary. After the usual disclaimers that it wasn’t our role to advise the new administration on appointments, we told Baker that Brady was the best man for the job.

I’ve always felt a bit guilty about boosting Brady in light of what happened to him outside the Washington Hilton on March 30, 1981. But we were right in what we said to Baker.

Jim Brady was the best man for the job.

SOURCE: Real Clear Politics

Lou Cannon covered the 1980 presidential campaign for The Washington Post and is a biographer of Ronald Reagan.
