Patricia Marx Offers Tips for the (Hopefully) College-Bound

August 2, 2014 Education

Patricia Marx, co-author of “Home Colleging (Because you Have no Choice).”
(Photo: ROBERT MAYER – HARVARD PHOTOGRAPHER)

Satirical author offers advice for getting into the right school

“If the Civil War made brothers fight brothers, the college application process makes parents fight children.”

So says Patricia Marx, a former writer for Saturday Night Live and Harvard Lampoon whose new book, Home Colleging (Because You Have No Choice), is a sendup of college manuals. In this book (co-authored with Sarah Payne Stuart), the “campus” is a “colonial that could use some paint,” because the slacker kid remains at home.

Marx offers some advice on how to avoid that student’s fate:

What do you think is the No. 1 mistake people make when searching for a college?

“I know Sarah (Stuart) believes you shouldn’t listen to your parents. I’m not sure I believe that. Having observed the process with my niece and nephew, I think kids should reach for the stars a little bit, especially now when it’s so easy to apply to college. They should certainly apply to a safety school, but they should dream a little. So they can be disappointed.”

What do you think about the community service component?

“I imagine it doesn’t have as much punch as it used to. When I was applying to college, the only kids who did community service did it because they were reducing their prison sentence. Now it’s de rigueur. Everybody builds a city in a lesser-developed country. I live in New York City, where money is no question for some kids. You hear about parents buying their children a country so they can say they were president of something.”

Is it better to know what you want to study going in, or is it OK to loaf around for a couple of years and figure it out later?

“I think If you’re a kid whose — to use an overused word, which college kids should never put on their essays — whose passion is to play tuba or to study epigenetics, and you’ve known that since you were 6, you should say that and you should probably do that. On the other hand, I don’t think that most kids know what they want to do, and it’s a shame if they limit themselves. I went to college not knowing what I was going to do. I picked my freshmen classes so that I was pre-everything. And at the end of four years, I’d eliminated everything. So the only thing I could really do was go to graduate school. Look, college is four years of kind of doing whatever you want to do, intellectually. And you should take advantage of that. Nothing you do will ever cost that much again — even if you buy an expensive car.”
