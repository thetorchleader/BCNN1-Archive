Oldest Black Baptist Church In Charlotte, North Carolina Celebrates 145 Years

August 5, 2012 Uncategorized

First Baptist-West looks toward its future while remembering the past
Some of Cedric Jones’ earliest memories of First Baptist Church-West are the underside of the pews he explored during services – and the palm of his father’s hand that awaited his rear when he resurfaced.
Jones, now pastor of Mount Zion Baptist Church in Philadelphia, gave the guest sermon for “Friends and Family Sunday,” a highlight of his old church’s yearlong 145th anniversary celebration.
Appropriately, he used the Bible he received for his baptism at First Baptist-West in 1973.
His theme: serving God and building community. The church has been doing plenty of both since 1867.
Charlotte’s oldest black Baptist congregation was born in an across-town balcony. Sixty-six former slaves grew weary of being forced to the back of First Baptist Church, of being treated, Jones said, “like something other than a son of God.”
The new congregation first met on Morehead Street near what is now the Dowd YMCA. The members then moved to South Church Street, where they eventually began building their own church. It took 10 years, the men working at night under the torches held by their mothers, wives and daughters.
Now situated on Oaklawn Avenue off Beatties Ford Road, the “mother church” of the city’s black Baptist congregations faces the same struggles as many other spiritual communities: The economy drains contributions. The membership shrinks. As older members die out, younger replacements bring in a less-binding commitment to the church.
During his sermon, Jones talked repeatedly of the pitfalls of “living unto ourselves,” a theme First Baptist-West pastor Dr. Ricky Woods had stressed during an earlier conversation about the church’s future.
“We can no longer be inwardly focused,” said Woods, who has led the Charlotte church since 1995.
“This can’t be about institutional survival. It has to be how we as people of faith not only make the church better but make the community better, too.”
Talk of the future notwithstanding, it wouldn’t be a birthday party without some reminiscing.
Church historian Queen Byrd remembers surfacing in the baptismal font, gasping for air but still feeling safe because she was held by then-pastor, the Rev. J.H. Moore. It was 1936. She was 5.
Sunday, the 81-year-old greeted arriving families in the church lobby.
Mildred Grier goes back even further. She’s 89, yet she remembers word for word the rhyming welcome she helped give each year on Children’s Day. Here’s how it ended:
Dear friends, our little songs and verses
We render in his name…,
Who long ago from heaven
To save the children came
Once upon a time, First Baptist-West was known as an upper-crust, “silk-stocking” congregation – the wheel, as someone once told Queen Byrd, not the spokes.
