WATCH: David Oyelowo says that Even Before Oscar Snub, he Felt that Blacks Are Only Celebrated and Awarded When They Appear in ‘Subservient’ Roles

February 2, 2015 Black

David Oyelowo attends the Virtuosos Award at the 30th Santa Barbara International Film Festival at the Arlington Theater on Feb. 1 in Santa Barbara, Calif. (Photo: Mark Davis, Getty Images for The Santa Barba)

Actor David Oyelowo, who failed to receive an Oscar nomination for his work as Martin Luther King in Selma, says there’s a simple reason why he was overlooked: The Academy doesn’t reward blacks in positions of power onscreen.

“Generally speaking, we, as black people, have been celebrated more for when we are subservient, when we are not being leaders or kings or being in the center of our own narrative, driving forward,” Oyelowo said at the Santa Barbara International Film Festival on Sunday. He continued, saying that over the years, there have been a number of deserving black actors in powerful roles that went unrewarded by the Academy.

“To me, Denzel Washington should have won for playing Malcolm X … (Sidney Poitier) wasn’t even nominated for In the Heat of the Night. He won for Lilies of the Field. So, this bears out what I’m saying, which is we’ve just got to come to the point whereby there isn’t a self-fulfilling prophecy – a notion of who black people are – that feeds into what we are celebrated as, you know, not just in the Academy, but in life generally. We have been slaves, we have been domestic servants, we have been criminals, we have been all of those things. But we’ve been leaders, and kings – those who changed the world. Those films where that is the case, are so hard to get made.”

Even making a film about an icon like MLK proved challenging because of Hollywood’s bias and insistence that white people be the main agents of momentum in stories about black people, Oyelowo said.

“People have often said to me, ‘Why has it taken so long?’ I mean, (King) was assassinated almost 50 years ago. There has been no film where Dr. King has been the center of his own narrative up until now. That’s because up until 12 Years a Slave and The Butler did so well, both critically and at the box office, films like this were told through the eyes of white protagonists because there is a fear of white guilt, so you have a very nice white person who holds black people’s hands through their own narrative. We don’t want to see that pain again, so you don’t really go into what that pain was in an authentic way. Both of those things are patronizing to the audience. You can’t have people curating culture in this way when we need to see these things in order to reform from them.”

Oyelowo added that Selma was greenlit only after 12 Years a Slave and The Butler, which he also starred in, did well at the box office in 2013. “Up until now, it’s been so hard to get these films made, but now they’re doing well internationally and critically and otherwise.”
