Antarctic Sea-level Rising Faster than Normal

September 2, 2014 Science

According to a report from the University of Southampton, UK, following a study of satellite data from the past 19 years, researchers found that fresh water from melting glaciers has caused the sea level around the coast of Antarctica to rise by 2 centimeters more than the global average of 6 centimeters.

The complete research findings appear in the latest issue of the journal Nature Geoscience.

The researchers detected the rapid rise in sea level by studying satellite scans of a region that spans over one million square kilometers. Antarctic ice sheet melting and the thinning of floating ice shelves has contributed a surplus of around 350 gigatonnes of freshwater to the surrounding ocean. This has led to a decrease in the salinity of the surrounding oceans, which has been corroborated by ship-based studies of the water.

“Freshwater is less dense than salt water and so in regions where an excess of freshwater has accumulated we expect a [localized] rise in sea level,” said Craig Rye, lead author of the paper. “The computer model supports our theory that the sea-level rise we see in our satellite data is almost entirely caused by freshening (a reduction in the salinity of the water) from the melting of the ice sheet and its fringing ice shelves.”
