Before Supreme Court, States Defend Gay Marriage Bans as Democratic Actions

March 27, 2015 National

Four Midwest states defended their gay marriage bans Friday, a month before the Supreme Court is to hear their case.
(Photo: Jose Luis Magana, AP)

The four states seeking to maintain bans against same-sex marriage told the Supreme Court Friday that the democratic process should decide the issue, not judicial dictate.

In their briefs responding to gay and lesbian couples, widowers and children challenging those bans, the states of Michigan, Ohio, Kentucky and Tennessee contended that the Constitution’s 14th Amendment — guaranteeing “equal protection” and “due process” — does not define marriage.

“The 14th Amendment does not dictate any particular marriage view, so the courts are not in a position to impose one,” Michigan Attorney General Bill Schuette argued. “Voters have a legitimate interest in promoting their own view, and while not all voters agree, the marriage view adopted since before the country’s founding is constitutional.”

Several of the briefs specifically targeted Justice Anthony Kennedy, who has provided the decisive fifth vote in previous gay rights cases and who wrote the high court’s 5-4 decision in 2013 striking down a key part of the Defense of Marriage Act. The Michigan brief begins citing him on page 2; the Ohio brief quotes from his opinions several times.

“The Constitution’s structure requires a stability which transcends the convenience of the moment,” Ohio Attorney General Michael DeWine said, directly quoting Kennedy’s opinion in an earlier case.

The four cases, combined for oral argument April 28, stem from the only federal appeals court in the nation to uphold state bans against same-sex marriage. Challengers from Michigan and Kentucky are seeking to marry in their home states. Those from Ohio and Tennessee, as well as some Kentucky plaintiffs, want their marriages from other states recognized by their home states.

The challengers filed their briefs in late February, and within a week more than 70 friend-of-the-court briefs were filed by proponents of same-sex marriage. Now that process is being reversed; dozens of briefs from gay marriage opponents are expected during the coming week.

The justices will address two questions during oral argument, devoting 90 minutes to the basic question of whether the 14th Amendment requires states to marry gay as well as straight couples, and 60 minutes to whether they must recognize marriages from other states.

Thirty-seven states and the District of Columbia permit same-sex marriage — more than half of them because of court orders as opposed to democratic processes. Thirteen states still ban the practice.
