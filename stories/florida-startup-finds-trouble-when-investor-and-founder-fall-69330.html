Florida Startup Finds Trouble When Investor and Founder Fall Out

April 23, 2015 You Can Work...

© Provided by The Wall Street Journal.

Investors often wield tremendous influence over a startup—and that can backfire if the relationship between investor and entrepreneur turns bitter.Just ask Virginia Barnett, of Lithia, Fla. Last year, she co-founded Gr8code, a business that offered bootcamp-style computer programming courses for beginners.

A former Walt Disney Co. guest services agent, she said she ran the startup’s day-to-day operations—until a key investor stepped in and took over.

The investor ousted her from management, she said, and “hijacked” the company. “This was my idea,” the 46-year-old Ms. Barnett said, referring to the original business. “I was really hurt.”

The investor, Steve Brickner, said through his lawyer that Ms. Barnett was an inept manager who put the training business and his investment in jeopardy. Mr. Brickner’s OmniElite Financial Group says he has two decades experience in owning and operating companies.

Tensions can arise when wealthy individuals known as angel investors, believe they “have a right” to be involved with the management of a startup, because their money is at stake, said Brian Cohen, managing founder of New York Venture Partners and an angel investor.

Entrepreneurs may be focused on creating the perfect product or service, but their investors typically are more concerned about seeing a return on their investment, said Jerome Katz, the Coleman Foundation Professor of Entrepreneurship at St. Louis University.
