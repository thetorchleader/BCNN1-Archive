How Will Religion Affect the 2016 Presidential Race? Religious Leaders Offer Opinions

May 29, 2015 Opinion

The next United States presidential election will be held on Tuesday, November 8, 2016.

In addition to the economy, foreign policy, income inequality, terrorism, same-sex marriage, gun control, health care, campaign finance, marijuana policy, etc., many expect religion and issues related to religion to figure prominently in the campaign. Will the religion and denomination of the president matter? Will a religious president be more effective in improving the moral quality of the nation? Will the issue of religiosity be top on the minds of the voters?

We asked our panel of distinguished religious leaders of the region the following question: Will religion make a significant impact in the outcome of the 2016 U.S. presidential election?

Here is what they have to say:

POLITICS, NOT RELIGION

Bradley S. Corbin, Bahá’í teacher

The next president of the United States will be Christian – simply because no other religion is represented by the current candidates. Catholics lead the list, followed by Presbyterians, then Methodists. I don’t believe the average American cares enough about the differences in these Christian denominations to choose a president solely on that basis. Also, none of the candidates could possibly endorse the beliefs of their religion on all issues; so the public will chose by political partisanship and the issues the candidate personally supports. If there is a religion that will figure prominently in the campaign it is the “religion” of money, power, and control; for a presidential candidate to be a contender, they must be endorsed and brokered by its members, the top 1 percent of our population. America is a great country; we deserve a president who embodies equality, honesty, and unity. If only we could find one.

WORLDVIEW MATTERS

Stephen B. Bond, senior pastor of Summit Christian Church, Sparks

Yes, religion will matter in the upcoming presidential election. Religion always matters because it informs the voters about the worldview of the candidates. Those with an areligious worldview exclude God from their understanding of the meaning of life and morality. Those with a religious worldview believe that God is central to their understanding of the meaning of life and morality.

These two worldviews are increasingly colliding in our society today. This is one reason America is so deeply divided. This division appears to be exacerbated by the increasing polemics which accompany each new election. I suspect the 2016 presidential race will be the most vitriolic in history.

I believe the only hope is for God to bring widespread revival throughout our nation. Only God’s grace can transform the human heart in a way that will allow the tensions to subside and civil discourse to replace the rancor.

WON’T MATTER

Kenneth G. Lucey, UNR philosophy/religion professor

Anyone interested in the 2016 presidential election cannot do better than to consult Nate Cohn’s regular column entitled “The Upshot” in The New York Times. Particularly relevant to today’s question is his column of May 13 entitled “A Big Decline in Americans Identifying as Christian.” Cohn reports on an extensive survey of 35,000 adults by the Pew Research Center. He reports that 71 percent of American adults report themselves to be Christians and notes that this is a decline of five millions adults since a similar survey in 2007. The drop represents a generational change as many non-Christian millennials reach adulthood. A previous column of Cohn’s from April 9, 2015 spelled out in detail how party candidates actually get picked by mainline party leaders during what he calls “The Invisible Primary.” That column described precisely why religion is now considered irrelevant to the candidate selection process.

IT SHOULD NOT BUT IT WILL

Soha S. Elfass, Northern Nevada Muslim Community youth member

People used to vote for presidential candidates whose views are generally aligned with theirs. Thus religion used to play some role in these elections because it is expected that religion has an impact on one’s views, which can be translated as policies if elected. However, nowadays religion and denomination are becoming major factors in presidential elections — not just because of the moral values alone; people are becoming biased towards the religion or the denomination of the candidate. If you do not believe that, then ask yourself what are the chances of a Muslim candidate? This is unfortunate because this way our country can lose a candidate who would have been a great leader just because he or she belongs to a certain religion or denomination. Voters should be objective and elect the best candidate for the job regardless of their religious affiliation. However, the 2016 election will be affected by religion, no doubt.

VOTE FOR UNITY

Robert W. Chorey, Roman Catholic Diocese of Reno curia moderator

It seems that religion has always been a part of U.S. politics, whether used for or used against candidates. I think that we voters want to know the moral underpinnings of our candidates.

Catholics, ideally, use moral principles to guide our choices in elections. Yet we realize that no one candidate ever encompasses our complete moral view; and we find it difficult to agree on which candidate best reflects our principles. Sadly, division happens because of this.

Still, we believe that the will of God is unity. So that desire for unity needs to be in our hearts as we discern and vote; and how we engage with one another, whether Christian, Jew, Muslim, Buddhist or atheist. We need to use our religious beliefs to see beyond our own selves and even our political party, and envision a wider community.
