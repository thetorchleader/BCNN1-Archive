Black Churches in Ohio Preach Jesus and Push for Early Voting in Sunday Morning Services

October 6, 2014 Black

Pastor Raymond Bishop stands with one of his church’s vans at Mount Pilgrim Baptist Church at Hoag and Dorr streets. His church offers rides to voters, especially senior citizens and the poor. Mount Pilgrim has been a common campaign stop for politicians.
THE BLADE/JETTA FRASER

Church has always played a big role in African-American politics.

And the onset of early voting on Sundays has made the church, its pulpits, and its vans even more significant.

It’s even got a name: “Souls to the Polls.”

The close relationship of black churches and politics, typically Democratic politics, was part of the basis of the lawsuit about Ohio voting that reached the U.S. Supreme Court last week.

The Rev. Raymond Bishop, pastor of Mount Pilgrim Baptist Church, is one of the most active practitioners of “Souls to the Polls.”

“It’s something most African-American churches have done forever without having a particular title to it,” he said. “Before Sunday voting, even if it were a Tuesday election, churches would still admonish persons and engage souls to the polls, providing information and transportation.”

He said poor people and senior citizens are more likely to need a ride, and that’s what the church provides.

Mr. Bishop allows politicians to speak to the congregation from his pulpit, and he defended the practice, saying blacks have fewer venues in which to engage with politicians than white voters.

“The difference is the white community has the ability to engage in political dialogue at country clubs and golf courses. We do it at church,” he said.

Politicians typically don’t speak from the pulpits of white churches, whereas it is a regular practice in Toledo for politicians to make the rounds of black churches on the Sundays before to the election.

Sometimes, ministers leave little doubt as to who they want to win an election.

In 2013, former Toledo Mayor Mike Bell was recognized in the congregation at Jerusalem Missionary Baptist Church, where Pastor Willie Perryman, Jr., said, “I can’t tell you who to vote for. I can tell you who I’m going to vote for. That’s Mayor Mike Bell.”

Mayor Bell lost the election, and was replaced by fellow independent D. Michael Collins, who also had not neglected the African-American churches. He paid his respects at Mount Pilgrim, attending a fund-raising event after morning services the Sunday before the general election.
