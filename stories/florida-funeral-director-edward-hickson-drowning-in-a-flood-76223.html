Florida Funeral Director Edward Hickson ‘Drowning in a Flood of Young Black Men’; Sees Fallout of Black-On-Black Crime First Hand

May 28, 2015 Black

Edward Hickson

Edward Hickson is drowning in a flood of dead young black men.

Yet the man who owns Hickson Funeral Home here refuses to allow the bullet-riddled bodies to pull him under.

Hickson understands that the Dunbar neighborhood needs him. He is their grief bearer and calming presence; he restores dignity to those who lost it along the way.

And, though Hickson strong enough to shoulder the neighborhood’s death pains, he prays the bloody water recedes.

Yes, the senseless killings — two suspects accused in the drive-by shooting of 5-year-old Andrew Faust on Oct. 13 were released from jail Tuesday because the only official witness to the crime recanted — help Hickson provide for his family. But he would rather lose business than young lives.

Instead, black-on-black violent crime continues. So he continues to do what he learned from his father 35 years ago in Arcadia, Fla.

Hickson’s father, a second-generation funeral director, taught him how to care for the living and dead when he was 12 years old.

“Back then, there were no EMTs. So my father and I rushed to the scene, secured the person as best we could, then rushed them to the ER,” said Hickson, now 48.

Most deaths that Hickson and his father, Eugene Hickson, encountered resulted from natural causes or car accidents. Homicides were rare in the town about 50 miles north of Fort Myers, but one Edward Hickson hasn’t forgotten: A high school classmate’s throat was cut in a bar fight.

“That was tough to deal with,” he said. “We were good friends and played baseball together.”

Life soon returned to normal in the quiet rural town of about 5,800 people. Edward Hickson continued working with his father, did well in high school and fished often.

However, a phone call from Dunbar shattered the silence.

In the 1980s, the cocaine epidemic was laying waste to the predominantly black Dunbar neighborhood. Finley Carter, Dunbar’s prominent funeral director, needed help because of the number of drug-related killings.

He called Hickson’s father.
