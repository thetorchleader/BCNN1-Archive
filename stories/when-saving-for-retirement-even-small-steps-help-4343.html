When Saving for Retirement, Even Small Steps Help

March 10, 2012 Uncategorized

Money Watch, a personal finance column that runs every Saturday, features a financial planner from the National Association of Personal Financial Advisors answering
reader questions about saving, protecting and growing your money. To
submit a question, e-mail USA TODAY personal finance reporter Christine
Dugas at: cdugas@usatoday.com.

Q: If you’re a young person, how can you start
saving for retirement if your job doesn’t offer a 401(k) plan and you
don’t have much money after you pay your bills and student loans?

A:
It’s hard to know where to start saving when there are so many things
competing for your dollars. And sometimes looking at the enormity of how
much you “should” be saving can become so discouraging that you give up
before you start. Think progress, not perfection; even small steps
start you in the right direction and get your savings momentum going.

As
important as retirement savings is, an emergency fund is a crucial part
of a sound financial footing. Get into a habit of saving for both.
After your paycheck is automatically deposited into your checking
account, direct your bank to regularly transfer a certain amount of
money into your savings account for your safety net and another amount
into your retirement account.

Then you won’t have to think about it — except to record the transfers so you don’t overdraw your checking account.

If you’re just starting out, chances are you qualify for a Roth IRA, which is a great place to save for retirement (do check IRS
regulations before contributing). With a Roth IRA, you don’t get a tax
benefit now, but when it comes time to retire (age 59½ and beyond), your
qualified withdrawals are entirely tax free.

In
a traditional IRA you may get a tax deduction now, but the earnings on
your contributions will be taxed later. You also may withdraw your Roth
contributions (not the earnings) at any time without a penalty –although
it’s not ideal because you’ll need the money in retirement.

You also should look for a financial institution
that will allow you to open your account with a low or no minimum
contribution, such as TD Ameritrade.

Many
financial institutions can act as a custodian for your IRA, including
mutual fund companies and brokerage firms, which can offer a wide
variety of investments with low expense ratios and low commissions.

Developing
the discipline of savings is important. If $25 a month is all you can
spare, it’s a start. And by paying yourself first, automatically and
right off the top, chances are you won’t even miss it.

Erin A. Baehr,NAPFA-Registered Financial Adviser

Baehr Family Financial, Stroudsburg, N.J.
