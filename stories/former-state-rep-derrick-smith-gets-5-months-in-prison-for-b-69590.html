Former State Rep. Derrick Smith Gets 5 Months in Prison for Bribery, Corruption Charges

April 24, 2015 Black

Former state Rep. Derrick Smith, center, walks out of court with the Rev. Marvin Alexander, left, and attorney Sheldon Sorosky after being sentenced April 23, 2015, at the Dirksen U.S. Courthouse in Chicago for bribery and extortion. (Nancy Stone, Chicago Tribune)

Former state Rep. Derrick Smith wept in a federal courtroom Thursday as he described how his bribery conviction has brought public humiliation and cost him not only his budding political career but also the respect of his children and grandchildren.

But moments later, after a federal judge sentenced him to just five months in prison, Smith was all smiles.

“God bless you,” Smith said to his former constituents after back-slapping family members outside the courtroom. “It’s about rebuilding right now.”

Smith, 51, had faced up to about five years behind bars. A federal jury convicted him last June of one count each of bribery and extortion for pocketing a $7,000 bribe as part of an FBI sting.

Despite the lenient sentence, U.S. District Judge Sharon Johnson Coleman had tough words for Smith, the son of a precinct captain who rose from poverty to a series of public agency jobs that ultimately earned him an appointment to a West Side legislative seat in 2011 by Democratic leaders.

The judge noted that despite the long history of political corruption cases being brought in Illinois, Smith had been in office only briefly before he was caught taking the cash bribe — which Smith called “cheddar” in undercover recordings — from a trusted low-level campaign operative who was secretly working for the FBI. As part of the ruse, Smith was led to think that a day care operator in his district needed a letter of support from him for a state grant.

“It’s all on you, Mr. Smith,” Coleman said. “You decided to take the cheddar. You decided to betray your constituents, and you decided to squander your opportunity.”

But Coleman said the minimum of four years in prison that prosecutors requested would only add to the cost for taxpayers to prosecute Smith, a case the judge said “could be seen as overkill.”
