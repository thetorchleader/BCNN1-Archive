At McDonald’s Gospelfest 2013, Gospel Stars and Church Leaders Praise, Worship, and Uplift

May 14, 2013 Uncategorized

The kid would not turn over the Prudential Center stage. He was possessed, carried away by the force of his own praise, lost in a very passionate — and very loud — meditative trance.
Keith Bellamy of Asbury Park, strong of voice and spirit and, judging by his cane, either partially or fully blind, sang his hallelujahs over and over, like the salvation of his 9-year-old soul depended upon it. McDonald’s Gospelfest producer and director A. Curtis Farrow rested a fatherly hand on Bellamy’s shoulder — a clear signal to his pupil that it was time to relinquish the microphone.
At an ordinary concert, this might have caused some embarrassment. But this was Gospelfest, an eight-hour encounter with the holy, featuring solo singers and choirs, praise dancers, rappers and poets, shoulder to shoulder on the Newark arena stage.
For 30 years, the McDonald’s-sponsored concert and competition has been providing a platform for inspiration and a conduit for the divine. As Bellamy sang, those in the crowd began to holler back. Some leapt to their feet; some clapped along; everybody recognized a soul aflame. Then the band began to get into it, punctuating his shouts with organ swells and cymbal crashes. The spirit leapt like an electric current through the congregation, and with it came the recognition that always accompanies the performances at Gospelfest: Genuine joy is not something easily contained, and when it strikes, it’s nothing but good news.
Gospel is the most overlooked form of American popular music. Reviewers at major publications ignore it; high-profile blogs barely mention it; commercial radio swerves around it. Yet contemporary pop is inconceivable without it. Many mainstream pop stars began singing as children in choirs; even those who did not often borrow the cadences of African-American church music. The call and response and audience participation segments so crucial to modern pop shows are pulled straight from the pulpit. Many of hip-hop’s most accomplished producers lean hard on gospel rhythms and gospel grandeur. And almost every form of pop music tries to elicit from listeners what gospel provides: an emotional release, an outpouring of feeling. The stars of Gospelfest — Aretha Franklin, Cissy Houston, Donnie McClurkin, John P. Kee, Bishop Hezekiah Walker, Smokie Norful and Lecrae — are experts at doing this, and sitting (but more usually standing) in their presence was a sustained thrill.
Eight hours of anything is exhausting, and even the reach toward transcendence would become wearying were gospel not as varied as it is. Modern gospel maintains firm contact with all of the popular forms it has touched: jazz, hip-hop, R&B, blues, even square Christian Contemporary pop. The modern minister of music — an increasingly powerful position in many churches — needs to be an artistic polymath, and a persuader of worshipers clinging to tradition. (Farrow himself is the minister of music at Newark’s New Hope Baptist Church, home of the Houstons and one of gospel’s foremost cathedrals.)
