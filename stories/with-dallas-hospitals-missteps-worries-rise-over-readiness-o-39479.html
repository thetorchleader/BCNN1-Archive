With Dallas Hospital’s Missteps, Worries Rise Over Readiness of Other Medical Facilities to Deal with Ebola

October 14, 2014 National

Texas Health Presbyterian Hospital Chief Clinical Officer Dr. Daniel Varga answers questions about a health care worker who provided hospital care for Thomas Eric Duncan who contracted Ebola, during a press conference at the hospital, Sunday, Oct. 12, 2014, in Dallas (Photo: Brandon Wade, AP)

Before the country’s first Ebola patient showed up at its emergency room, Texas Health Presbyterian Hospital Dallas was a respected, renowned hospital.

But a string of possible missteps in the wake of the Ebola crisis here has heaped negative publicity on the 898-bed facility and raised questions as to whether other, lesser-equipped hospitals are ready to deal with an Ebola outbreak.

Health care executives across the USA are closely monitoring developments at Texas Health Dallas as the medical staff treats its second Ebola patient in less than a month, hoping to learn from its successes as well as its errors. A hospital nurse, Nina Pham, 26, tested positive for the virus over the weekend and is being treated in isolation at the hospital.

A hospital spokeswoman declined to comment for this article.

The Ebola outbreak in Africa has killed more than 4,000 people and sickened more than 8,000, according to the World Health Organization. In the U.S., all eyes are on the Dallas hospital working to contain the virus.

“Everyone’s watching and everyone’s going to learn by their mistakes,” said Dr. Robert Murphy, a professor of medicine and biomedical engineering at Northwestern University’s Feinberg School of Medicine who has worked with infectious diseases in Africa.

Missteps and corrections made by Texas Health Presbyterian Hospital Dallas include:

Releasing Thomas Eric Duncan. The original Ebola patient checked himself into the hospital’s emergency room on Sept. 25 complaining of abdominal pain and fever and said he had recently been in Africa, signs that he may have had Ebola. After being checked by doctors, he was prescribed antibiotics, told to take Tylenol and released.

In an Oct. 2 statement, the hospital said Duncan had a temperature of “100.1” and that his symptoms were “not severe.” But medical records later provided to the Associated Press showed that Duncan had a high fever of 103 and that he had reported having “severe pain.” Staffers marked his fever with an exclamation point on his records, the Associated Press reported.

Duncan was readmitted to the hospital on Sept. 28, put in isolation and died there 10 days later.

Misinformation. Answering questions about Duncan’s initial visit, hospital officials first blamed a flaw in the electronic health records system for not allowing the information from the nurse treating Duncan to reach the appropriate doctors. A day later, they corrected that statement, saying there was no flaw.

That statement also said Duncan was confirmed to have Ebola on Sept. 29, later corrected to Sept. 30.

On Sunday, as news of Pham’s condition was made public, the hospital announced that it would be diverting all emergency room visits to other area hospitals. At 7:20 p.m., it announced it was no longer diverting its emergency visits. “The department is appropriately staffed, and ambulances may begin bringing patients in for care immediately,” it said.

Twenty-five minutes later, it reverted back to diversion status.
