Obama and Romney Fight Over Foreign Affairs

October 22, 2012 Uncategorized

Mitt Romney called for the U.S. to play a stronger role in world affairs and President Barack Obama said his opponent offered “wrong and reckless leadership” on Middle East policy, as the two met Monday for their third and final debate.

With polls showing the presidential race deadlocked, the two men laid out their views of America’s role in the world at a time when many voters are taking a last look at the candidates before the Nov. 6 election, two weeks away.

The debate, held at Lynn University in Boca Raton, Fla., was a far more sedate discussion than their debate a week earlier. The candidates sat at desks and interrupted each other only occasionally, in contrast to the aggressive confrontations of their prior meeting.

Still, Mr. Obama, who has lost the lead he held until recently in many polls of the presidential race, struck a combative stance. He immediately launched a harsh critique of Mr. Romney, saying he had taken inconsistent and misguided positions on the war in Afghanistan, Libya and other international issues.

“I know you haven’t been in a position to execute foreign policy,” Mr. Obama said, describing the contrast between the two men as “strong and steady leadership, not wrong and reckless leadership all over the map.”

“Attacking me is not an agenda,” Mr. Romney said. “Attacking me is not talking about how we’re going to deal with the challenges that exist in the Middle East and take advantage of the opportunity there and stem the tide of this violence.”

Mr. Romney laid out an expansive vision of an American role in the world, but was cautious when detailing a role for the U.S. military. “My strategy’s pretty straightforward, which is to go after the bad guys, to kill them,” he said when asked about terrorism and the changing Middle East. He then talked about fighting extremism in the region by boosting economic development, education opportunities and “gender equality” in Middle East nations to fight extremism.

“We don’t want another Iraq. We don’t want another Afghanistan,” Mr. Romney said, drawing distance from the last GOP president, George W. Bush. “That’s not the right course for us.”

Mr. Obama said the United States had taken a leadership role in the changing Middle East, citing the U.S. participation in the successful effort to remove Libyan leader Moammar Gadhafi from power.

“I and Americans took leadership in organizing an international coalition that made sure that we were able to—without putting troops on the ground, at the cost of less than what we spent in two weeks in Iraq—liberate a country that had been under the yoke of dictatorship for 40 years, got rid of a despot who had killed Americans,” he said.

Mr. Romney passed up an opportunity at the start of the debate to continue his pointed criticism of Mr. Obama’s handling of the attack last month on a U.S. consulate in Benghazi, Libya, that killed four Americans, including the U.S. ambassador. Instead, the Republican candidate, who has repeatedly raised questions about Libya on the campaign trail, talked more broadly about tackling the challenges in the Middle East.

“I congratulate him on taking out Osama bin Laden and going after the leadership al Qaeda. But we can’t kill our way out of this mess,” Mr. Romney said. “What we are seeing is a pretty dramatic reversal of the hopes we had for the region.”

Mr. Obama argued that Mr. Romney was more hawkish than he has portrayed himself, an argument similar to the one the president has made in recent days about Mr. Romney’s more centrist tone on social and other domestic policies.

When the discussion turned to Iran, Mr. Romney said he would take a harder line than has Mr. Obama on sanctions by trying to close loopholes, further isolate Tehran diplomatically, and indict Iran’s president for inciting “genocide.” He suggested the Obama administration’s early attempts to increase negotiations with Tehran were a mistake. “It is essential for a president to show strength from the beginning,” he said.
