“On Highway 61” Explores How Under-Sung Black Musicians Had a Huge Influence on American Music

January 13, 2015 Books

From Counterpoint, “On Highway 61,” hardcover, $28.00. (Counterpoint)

There’s this story about a man who’s stuck in the doldrums until an African-American comes along and, and through his or her exuberant lifeforce, manages to pull him out of it. The man is usually white, and while other dynamics can be switched around — woman instead of man, comedy or drama — the element of someone considered supremely normal is integral to this familiar narrative of supremely normal-enhancing otherness.

Dennis McNally’s “On Highway 61” is an epic tour of how, from the country’s inception, black people have directly influenced progressive notions of humanity and freedom, along with almost every form of music that’s appreciated as being American. He works his way through history using unique figures as both markers and forerunners to his own insights. For McNally, Henry David Thoreau is the first real American voice of dissent against a culture of profiteering prospered, in part, by an easily exploitable idea: McNally’s reference to “Cotton Mather’s notion that God’s favor could be shown in riches” seems to be the essence of it.

At a certain point, riches upon riches upon riches isn’t possible without screwing over vast swaths of people, or hell–make it people whose humanity you disavow, and you’ve got what McNally describes as a slavery that was “surely among the among the most absolute in human history.” One of the distinctions of U.S. slavery was just how much it othered the enslaved Africans, completely disavowing any timbers of humanity presupposed by the already horrid indentured servitude that many societies inflicted upon their members.

McNally sees Thoreau as a guiding light in this time. Compared to his peers, certainly, but Thoreau’s ideas were both lofty and skewed. In response to a culture that was moving even further from the pretense of the ideals of the country’s founders, Thoreau offered up a slightly ridiculous notion connecting enlightenment with a return to the pastoral in “Walden”; he also wrote what would essentially become one of the guiding texts for those who were denied civil rights. And yet, according to McNally, Thoreau’s 1845 journal entry (and subsequently his feeling that), “‘The degradation [sic] & suffering of the black man will not have been in vain if they contribute thus indirectly to give a loftier tone to the religion and politics of this country,’ is patronizing and insensitive to human suffering …”

That dynamic, however, is central to one of the book’s themes, which is put much more empathetically by McNally when he writes: “Where does an interest in the culture of the Other cross from interest to exploitation, from admiration to a demeaning worship, from curiosity to the worship of the exotic, implicitly another form of domination?”

Music, already important to West African cultures, was one of the ways that the enslaved kept their sanity. With black people converting to Christianity, the resulting spirituals grew from their unique traditions, which included an improvisation that took new influences along with them into something elevated beyond confines. The result made Protestant hymns into something that was, McNally writes, “highly rhythmic, percussive, and syncopated, and often featured call-and-response patterns–a perfectly synthesized form.”

Beyond producing music that was incredibly moving, those who were enslaved would find ways to mix their traditional dancing with mocking imitations of their slavers, whom found all of these things impressive enough to imitate themselves. The white imitation of black musical forms and black life was minstrelsy, which McNally asserts as the first exposure to black music for the majority of white Americans. McNally writes that “the minstrel show was created by white working-class showmen as a moneymaking exercise that gave their working-class largely Irish audiences someone lower on the social totem pole to mock,” but that there was this growing admiration for the music.

African-Americans would come to be minstrelsy’s most artful performers, earning a living at the high cost of enacting soul-shrinking stereotypes they didn’t create. Minstrelsy would incorporate a musical form that would blossom into ragtime, an African-American innovation on American marching band music. Branching off from the same improvisatory heart at the center of spiritual and ragtime would be the blues, jazz and rhythm and blues, now conventionally known as rock ‘n’ roll, with all its various subsets. All would be considered a bad influence by any variation on polite or respectable society, though this couldn’t deride the popular appeal of music less anemic than the classical, respectable variety — and that spoke far more deeply to the human condition.

McNally is keenly aware of the power of prevailing art had in influencing the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: he cites Mark Twain’s barbs at Walter Scott’s work, which influenced Southern notions of honor, with the latter’s books about a romanticized and dispossessed royalty who were much more noble and true than the corrupt tyrannical monarchy who oppressed them. Such constructs, along with its references to a nineteenth society increasingly obsessed with social climbing, make “On Highway 61” feel very contemporary.

The book also offers up some great insight into the field of music scholars and how their skewed views disassociated or trivialized African-American influences on what’s now considered “white” music. Even though it’s become quite disassociated from anything beyond Appalachian music, the banjo is very much an African-created instrument; any early banjo player that white scholars later idolized almost certainly learned from the African-Americans who innovated it. These scholars were at some of the most elite — and subsequently considered “best” — colleges in the country; their legacy includes taking the huge African-American influence on folk music out of the scholarly folk canon.

“On Highway 61” also explores the contributions of titans like Bessie Smith, Muddy Waters and Howlin’ Wolf, along with meticulous references to many under-sung black musicians who would end up having a huge influence on the likes of the Beatles and the Rolling Stones.
