91-Year-Old Sehon Williams of Columbia, MO Is Still the Backbone of his Family and Community

June 16, 2014 Black

Sehon Williams has been an important part of Columbia’s black community. While raising his family in Columbia, he worked hard to shelter his children from the ugliness of racism while confronting it in his own life. ¦ T.J. THOMSON

The year was 1946, but Sehon Williams still remembers standing in the bedroom on Switzler Street that he shared with his wife, Dora, and watching her in labor with their third child.

His first two children, both daughters, had been born while he was in the Army fighting in World War II. This time, he would be right there.

But as his wife’s pains became more intense, Williams felt a little useless. It was a rare moment of helplessness for a man who somehow always found a way forward.

Now 91 years old, Williams is father of six, grandfather of 14, great-grandfather of 16 and great-great-grandfather of two. Raising a family at a time of unconcealed discrimination, Williams said he strived to shelter his family from the ugliness of racism, and when he encountered it in his own life, he confronted it. Resilience became his trademark.

Although his name isn’t well known outside Columbia’s black community, many in the city can attest to his powerful influence.

“He’s a man of few words,” said Yvonne Matthews, who has attended church with Williams for years at St. Paul A.M.E. “But when he opens his mouth to say something, everyone pays attention.”

At his home on Noble Court, where he has lived since the 1960s, Williams keeps many scrapbooks stuffed full of letters, photographs, invitations and newspaper clippings in a wood chest that sits against a blue wall in his living room. He doesn’t bring them out often, but if you ask him, he’ll show you his treasures. He seems to have kept everything.

Fighting for his country

One letter he didn’t keep came to his parents’ house in Columbia in September 1942. He’d expected it but didn’t want it. It notified the 19-year-old, Lincoln University freshman he’d been drafted into the Army to serve in World War II.

The notice came on the heels of his wedding to Dora. They’d known each other since they were kids, and after a one-year courtship, the two were married Sept. 8, 1942. Williams shipped out two months later.

“I was heartbroken,” Williams said. “I didn’t want to leave my family.” But the new husband had little choice. He recalls having written to his new wife every day.

Stationed in a small Italian village, Williams was quartered in an abandoned water and light plant that had lost its roof during a bombing raid. It was just behind the front lines and vulnerable to flying shrapnel.

His outfit, the 370th Regimental Combat Team of the 92nd Infantry Division, is the celebrated African-American unit known as the Buffalo Soldiers — the only black infantry division to see combat in Europe during World War II. It was a segregated unit.
