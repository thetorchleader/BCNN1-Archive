Wall Street Journal: Southern Baptists Pick Black Leader

June 19, 2012 Uncategorized

Election by the Historically White Denomination–Founded By Abolition Opponents–Comes After a Long Transformation 

The Southern Baptist Convention elected its first black president Tuesday, marking a historic evolution for a denomination that was founded 167 years ago to defend slavery but now seeks to broaden the group’s appeal.
The Rev. Fred Luter Jr., a 55- year-old pastor in New Orleans, was elected to head the cooperative body of more than 45,000 churches, whose more than 16 million members include just about a million blacks.
“To God be the glory for the things that he has done,” a weeping Mr. Luter said at the annual meeting of the denomination in New Orleans, as thousands of Baptists cheered and clapped.
Mr. Luter hugged outgoing president Bryant Wright, wiped his eyes with a handkerchief and pointed upward. Mr. Wright called the election, which was unchallenged and unanimous, “a key moment in history.”
The election would have been inconceivable to slave owners and others who founded the denomination in 1845 in Augusta, Ga., to break from abolitionist Northern Baptist churches. After the Civil War, many black Baptist churches joined their own convention.
But faced with declining membership, the denomination is working to increase participation from its black and Hispanic congregations and plans soon to look into making overtures to Asian-Americans, Frank S. Page, the president and chief executive of the denomination’s executive committee, told the meeting.
All racial and ethnic groups must play a greater role in the denomination, he said, adding, “I see this as just the first step in a long road.” “We want to seek to deepen their involvement.”
So eager is the SBC to attract nonwhites it is considering allowing member churches to drop the term “Southern Baptists” because of its association with the Confederacy, and call themselves “Great Commission Baptists,” a Biblical reference to Jesus’s command to his disciples to spread his teachings throughout the world. The annual meeting voted Tuesday afternoon on the move but the results weren’t immediately known.
American churches as a whole remain segregated, with more than 90% of congregations having at least 80% of their membership from one race, said Michael Emerson, a sociologist at Rice University and an expert on race and religion.
Many Southern Baptists welcomed Mr. Luter’s appointment. “God was never interested in the color of the skin,” said Chris Fowler, music director of the Romar Beach Baptist Church in Orange Beach, Ala. “He was interested in the soul of the person,” said Mr. Fowler, who is white.
David Crosby, the white senior pastor of First Baptist Church of New Orleans, who nominated Mr. Luter, said many Baptist leaders believed Mr. Luter’s election was divinely guided.
Mr. Luter has been pastor of Franklin Avenue Baptist Church 26 years. When he began, the church had about 65 members, most of them white. Shortly before Hurricane Katrina, it had more than 7,000 members. Today the church has about 5,000, most of whom are black.
