The Clergy Who Inspired the Rev. Martin Luther King Jr.’s “Letter From Birmingham Jail”

April 12, 2013 Uncategorized

Fifty years ago today, April 12, 1963, eight clergy asked the Rev. Martin Luther King Jr. to delay civil rights demonstrations in Birmingham.
That same day, King was arrested and put in the Birmingham Jail. On April 16, King began writing his “Letter From Birmingham Jail,” directed at those eight clergy who were considered moderate religious leaders.
His epic response still echoes through American history.
King’s letter eloquently stated the case for racial equality and the immediate need for social justice. “I had hoped,” King wrote at one point, “that the white moderate would understand that the present tension in the South is a necessary phase of the transition from an obnoxious negative peace, in which the Negro passively accepted his unjust plight, to a substantive and positive peace, in which all men will respect the dignity and worth of human personality. Actually, we who engage in non-violent direct action are not the creators of tension. We merely bring to the surface the hidden tension that is already alive. We bring it out in the open, where it can be seen and dealt with.”
King started writing it from his jail cell, then polished and rewrote it in subsequent drafts, addressing it as an open letter to the eight Birmingham clergy who asked him to delay demonstrations. The letter was distributed to the media, published in newspapers and magazines in the months after the Birmingham demonstrations, and it appeared in his book, “Why We Can’t Wait,” in 1964. The eight clergy have been pilloried in history for their stance.
King’s letter, with its criticism of the white clergy opposition, made them look as if they were opposed to the civil rights movement.
But their positions were more nuanced than that, said Samford professor Jonathan Bass, whose 2001 book, “Blessed are the Peacemakers,” focuses on the writing of King’s letter and the personal stories of the eight clergy King addressed.
In January 1963, those same clergy had signed a letter in response to Gov. George Wallace’s harsh segregationist rhetoric, warning it could lead to violence.
“They were widely hailed for being among the most progressive religious leaders in the South,” Bass said. “They got a ton of hate mail from segregationists. All of them were harassed because of that statement.”
In 2001, Samford University gave an honorary doctorate to the Rev. Earl Stallings, pastor of First Baptist Church of Birmingham from 1961-65, who was one of the eight clergy addressed by King in the letter. The Rev. John T. Porter, pastor of the historic black Sixth Avenue Baptist Church from 1961-99 and a friend of King, also received an honorary doctorate that day.
Both Stallings and Porter died in 2006.
Porter said in 2001 he understood the difficult historical position the white clergy group found itself in.
“I saw tremendous pain and agony in the white pulpit,” Porter said. “The pressure was on them pretty heavily. I’m sure they never thought they would provoke the reaction they did.”
