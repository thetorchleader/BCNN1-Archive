Virginia Governor Bob McDonnell Opens Up on the Witness Stand About his Rough 40-Year Marriage; says he Is Staying with his Pastor

August 21, 2014 Politics

McDonnell described a breakdown in his marriage that he attributed to his schedule. | AP Photo

His expression was grim, his voice cracked at just the right times, and he appeared in genuine pain — all while describing his wife.

As Bob McDonnell testified in his public corruption trial Thursday, it was easy to see why he was once regarded as one of the most talented Republican politicians in the country. He didn’t simply throw his wife, Maureen, under a bus to keep himself out of prison. He laid her down gently on the road, with a pillow and blanket.

As she looked on just a few feet away, McDonnell described a breakdown in his marriage that he attributed to his busy schedule as a rising politician, her stress over their increasingly visible role, tensions over her business selling dietary supplements, and what he considered her abusive treatment of her staff.

(Also on POLITICO: McDonnell: He and wife living apart)

He never did it in a glib, cocky way. The old “hit me with whatever you’ve got” McDonnell was gone. But he was definitely polished, and definitely careful. In other words, he did everything he could to gain the sympathies of a small, crucial group of voters: the jury.

McDonnell’s legal team has made it clear for weeks what its defense will be against the federal charges facing the former governor and his wife: They couldn’t have conspired to take gifts and loans from a businessman in exchange for special favors because their marriage was falling apart and they were barely on speaking terms.

To convince the jury of that, McDonnell laid out the painful details of his marriage, step by step, sounding by turns earnest and devastated.

When he said he scolded her, during his days as Virginia attorney general, for using a campaign list to help promote her “nutraceutical” products — dietary supplements that are supposed to provide health benefits — he was clearly depicting himself as the ethical one in the relationship. But he also tried to sound like the understanding husband: “I knew she was upset about that. It was her job. It was what she was good at.”

(Also on POLITICO: McDonnell describes ‘yelling’ wife)

When he acknowledged that, as governor, he started staying away from Maureen, he took a dramatic, deep breath before describing how he did that: “Frequently staying at the office later.”

He looked down grimly as he described the strain he felt when he urged his wife not to yell at her staff so much: “It hurt. She would remind me several times, ‘I’m not staff. I’m your wife.’”

And the moment McDonnell revealed that he and Maureen aren’t living together during the trial? His voice turned raspy, Bill Clinton-style: “I just didn’t have the emotional ability to rehash the things that had happened at the end of the day with Maureen.”

It sounded sincere — and maybe a lot of it was sincere. But it was also pitch-perfect for the jurors.

Unfortunately for McDonnell, he didn’t always hit the perfect notes when talking about the money his family received from Jonnie Williams, the businessman to whom the couple allegedly traded favors for gifts and loans.

When defense attorney Henry Asbill asked him about the $15,000 Williams gave to Cailin McDonnell, the former governor’s daughter, for her wedding in 2011, he didn’t show any of the moral outrage he had described during his testimony about his other ethical disagreements with Maureen.

(Also on POLITICO: McDonnell’s sister defends ex-gov)

“I had to think about it,” McDonnell said. But he concluded that Williams was a rich guy who could easily afford it, and “if Cailin wanted to accept it and put the money toward the wedding, that was probably OK.”
