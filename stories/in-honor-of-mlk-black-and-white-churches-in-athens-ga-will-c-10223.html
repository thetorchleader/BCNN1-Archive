In Honor of MLK, Black and White Churches in Athens, GA Will Combine Their Services

January 19, 2013 Uncategorized

To honor the legacy of America’s greatest civil rights leader, a traditionally white church and a traditionally black church intend to again come together in worship.


Pastor Edward R. Bolen, left, of Milledge Avenue Baptist Church, and Reverend Abraham Mosley of Mount Pleasant Baptist Church, prepare for an upcoming combined worship service in Athens, Ga., on Thursday, January 17, 2013. The service will be held at Milledge Avenue Baptist Church on Jan. 20, 2013 in honor of Martin Luther King, Jr. (Richard Hamm/Staff) OnlineAthens & The Athens Banner-Herald OnlineAthens/Athens Banner-Herald
Mt. Pleasant Baptist Church and Milledge Avenue Baptist Church will bring their congregations together again on Sunday for a worship service in honor of Martin Luther King Jr. and his dream that people of all races would one day “be able to work together, to pray together, to struggle together, to go to jail together, to stand up for freedom together.”
This is the second year the churches intend to combine their worship services in honor of King. The idea was forged after Edward Bolen, senior pastor of Milledge Avenue Baptist Church, approached the Rev. Abraham Mosley of Mt. Pleasant Baptist Church last year about doing a pulpit exchange on King’s birthday. Mosley liked the idea, but thought that switching pulpits wouldn’t be enough because he said he still had too many black folks afraid of white folks in his church and that Bolen still had too many white folks afraid of black folks in his.
The two churches needed to join, Mosley said.
King often laced his sermons with the phrase “the most segregated time in America is at eleven o’clock on Sunday morning, and the most segregated school in America is Sunday school.”
Breaking that “segregation” became the challenge of both churches.
“The feedback was extremely positive last year, both within our respective congregations and throughout the community,” Bolen said. “We had a great turnout for the worship service and a tremendous percentage stayed for lunch.”
The lunch was well “integrated,” Bolen said because he and Mosley encouraged and challenged everyone to intentionally sit with members of the other congregation. Another dividend was the “I didn’t know you went to church here” conversations that were overheard. 
“Many folks from each congregation knew each other professionally and socially, but had never put a faith connection together,” Bolen said. “There also were some intentional friendships that were forged out of that day. We agreed on the spot that we wanted to do this again the next year.”
Bolen recalled that at last year’s service he was approached by an elderly black gentlemen during lunch who shook his hand and said, “This was a fine day. But realize, if you had told me 50 years ago that one of Athens’ prominent white churches would cancel their morning worship service and gather with us in our church building, I never would have believed it possible. This is God at work.”
“It gave me goose bumps,” Bolen said.  
In addition to the black and white members of each church interacting, Mosley and Bolen said the symbolic element of bringing two Baptist congregations together also serves to remind both congregations that they are King’s descendants as Baptists because King’s roots are as a young Baptist minister serving what was originally his community in Georgia.
The churches also hope the service will act as an educational opportunity to the younger generation that knows of the Civil Rights Movement and King only through the pages of history books.
By bringing together different generations, the service will give today’s younger generation a connection to their roots as Baptists in Georgia and help them realize they are part of the tradition of standing up for justice that King and others left behind.
