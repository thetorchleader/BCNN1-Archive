New York Times Managing Editor Admits Journalists Have an ‘Anti-Parenting Bias’

April 10, 2015 Media

Taking a walk in Wilhelmshaven, Germany.
Ingo Wagner/Picture-Alliance via, Associated Press

There is no shortage of discussion about media bias. Are we too liberal? Too protective of the establishment and too focused on the affluent? Too negative? Too sensationalist? I’ll leave those debates for others, but I do think the news media suffer from one form of bias that doesn’t get much attention.

We are biased toward paid work — and against time spent doing anything else, especially parenting.

Think about it: As journalists, we are, by definition, working. As is typical in white-collar jobs today, many journalists work long hours, missing meals with their children and spouses, homework sessions, school plays, sporting events or simply time spent lollygagging around the house. There are often good reasons: Many journalists love what they do, and the work can be important. But a day has only 24 hours, which requires trade-offs. Journalists often spend the bulk of their waking hours being journalists first — and fathers, mothers, husbands, wives, boyfriends, girlfriends, sons and daughters second.

Given these choices, you can understand why we might be eager to highlight evidence that spending time at the office doesn’t make us any worse at being fathers, mothers, husbands, wives and the like. This week brought a particularly stark example.

A new study by three sociologists found that the amount of time mothers spent with children and adolescents “did not matter” with regard to their behavior, emotions or academics. The study, as Justin Wolfers wrote for us this week, was based on scant data — just one weekday and one weekend day for each of the mothers being studied.

More rigorous research on parenting tends to be based on a month or more of behavior. By looking at only two days, the new study had a heavy dose of randomness. And randomness tends to lead to findings of no correlation. “It’s essentially a nonfinding,” as Justin wrote, “in that they failed to find correlations that could be reliably discerned from chance.”
