Rev. Dr. Cleo LaRue to Lecture on ‘What the Pulpit Needs Now is Love’ In Houston, October 20th

October 14, 2014 Black

Cleophus LaRue

In order for 21st century North American Christians to see the gospel clearly we need to look with the eyes of the oppressed and marginalized.

The Black church’s prophetic tradition is one such “lens” that can help us imagine God’s preferred future in the midst of empire. God’s invitation to comprehensive human flourishing invites us to imagine ourselves foremost as followers of the Jesus’ Way, and only secondarily as American. An image we reverse to the detriment of our faith.

Reading from the margins helps us to understand that ethics – our capacity to do what we know is right – is tied intimately with our sight, how we see the world and God’s action in it. Jesus insistent invitation is to join God’s narrative of bringing restoration to every street, system, soul. We have been created (Ephesians 2:10), redeemed (2 Corinthians 5:21) and called (John 20:21) to channel our best energies into the common good. For Jesus, the abundant life promised is the missional shalom life of making peace for the common good.

Reverend Dr. Cleophus (Cleo) LaRue is such a voice. And he’s coming to Houston on Monday October 20th to lecture on the importance of Jesus-centered preaching today. His lecture, What the Pulpit Needs Now is Love, is part of Healing the Brokenness’s Lunch series and will be held 11:30-1PM.

In his book I’ll believe I’ll testify, LaRue says about the dissonance between white and black theology, ”The paired down theology of the white western academy that was cut and shaved to fit a small scale universe was always a tight fit for the theological world of the black church.” (pg 51)

This is a moving book highlighting the best of black preaching (LaRue is himself an excellent preacher, and a professor of homiletics) and its gift to the American religious scene. He speaks highly about the love of language, art, and the importance of the imagination in black church preaching and theology. The imagination helps us to see and to say what often lies dormant within us. As Larue says, “bringing to conscious formation new, interesting, and creative ways of presenting images and ideas that embody, shape, illumine, clarify and render accessible the Word of God on a particular day to a particular people.” (pg 72).
