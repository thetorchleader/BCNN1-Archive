African-American Storytellers Sought for 450th Anniversary of Florida’s St. Augustine

August 28, 2014 Black

SUE.BJORKMAN@STAUGUSTINE.COM
Rize Cole, project coordinator for the African-American living history project, is seeking storytellers for the upcoming 450th. Interviews will be held Sept. 4 at the St. Johns Cultural Council Office.

Storytelling is one of history’s great oral traditions. Before radio, television or the internet, everything we needed to know was passed along by word-of-mouth. A good storyteller could so captivate an audience that the line between entertainment and education completely blurred.

These are the types of storytellers now being sought to participate in an African-American living history project planned for the 450th Anniversary of St. Augustine.

Interviews will be held from 11 a.m. to 1 p.m. and 6 to 8 p.m. Sept. 4 at the St. John’s Cultural Council Office, 15 Old Mission Ave. Participants should come prepared to tell a two-minute story on any appropriate subject.

“We’re looking for believability,” Project Coordinator Rize Cole said. “We want people who can tell a story that makes you want to know more. A great storyteller has passion for the subject so we’re captivated and listening to every word they say.”

She should know. A dynamic poet, storyteller and actress in her own right, Cole is perhaps best known as Miz Anna, an 18th century midwife re-enactor.

“I wanted to head this project because I’m a storyteller,” Cole said. “It’s what I love to do and I know how powerful it is. We have a chance here to affect some change in the historical perspective and help bring understanding.”

Her CD, “Spirit Voices Rizing,” is a collection of narratives and poetry inspired by the stories of runaway slaves. Cole has performed poetry readings at the Library of Congress in Washington, D.C., as well as in South America and the Caribbean.

Although storytelling is often full of fanciful folklore, this project will concentrate on true stories of the role of African-Americans in St. Augustine from the 1800s and beyond.

“It’s going to be very entertaining and fun, but we’re steering clear of fiction,” Cole said.

Organizers plan to work closely with local historians to compile in-depth, but rarely shared stories in a way that people will understand. Cole said the Civil Rights era has been well-documented here so they will not put their emphasis on that period. They hope to dig deeper to discover and share the lesser-told tales from the 18th and 19th centuries.
