Plans for Myrtle Beach Tuition-Free Seminary Fall Through Amid Questions

December 24, 2014 Education

A Christian college claiming to offer tuition-free higher education abruptly left its Myrtle Beach headquarters this month, just weeks before the institution was scheduled to hold its first classes.

Myrtle Beach Christian College and Pilgrim Theological Seminary, an independent and unaccredited nonprofit, sent out a news release on Nov. 25 touting a program that provides instruction from a Christian perspective at First United Methodist Church in Myrtle Beach. The release said classes would begin in January. School officials made follow-up calls to local media asking for coverage.

But since the announcement, the church has severed ties with the school, some instructors have withdrawn their support and the administrators who gave initial interviews about the program no longer work there.

“All hell broke loose,” said Hal Atkins, a Maryland minister who chairs the college’s board of governors. “We’re having a real spiritual battle.”

The challenge is also a financial one. Atkins confirmed Friday that the college has struggled to raise money to fund its operations. The school has not even provided final paychecks to its now former employees.

“It saddens me that we’re struggling,” he said. “We’re not able to pay staff like we need to.”

At the center of the startup college’s wrangling is an ex-con turned pastor named Keith Smith, who lives in the Pittsburgh area.

Smith, a 67-year-old who serves as the college’s dean of students, envisioned the seminary, solicited the help of local ministers and hired staff this fall.

Despite having no headquarters, board members or administrators left in the area, Smith insists he hasn’t given up on his goal of establishing a tuition-free campus in Myrtle Beach. His target date for opening is the fall of 2015, though he hopes some courses will be offered at local churches in the spring. In the meantime, he’ll be searching for a new location.

“We weren’t prepared,” he said. “I couldn’t get things going as fast as I’d hoped.”

Dean on the doorstep

Some of the local people who had been involved with the college say they knew little about Smith before he reached out to them.

“Keith really just showed up on my doorstep,” said Buddy Phillips, who pastors the 2,000-member First United Methodist Church in downtown Myrtle Beach.

Phillips said Smith first contacted him via email. He assumed Smith had gotten his name from someone connected to prison ministries because Phillips taught maximum security inmates while working for Spartanburg Methodist College in the late 1980s. Phillips called around to some faith-based organizations in other states, inquiring about Smith. Those groups were vaguely familiar with his efforts to educate prisoners, he said.

Phillips insists Smith never tried to mask his background.

Smith was convicted of mail and wire fraud in Pennsylvania and received a 21-month prison sentence, according to the Federal Bureau of Prisons. He served one year and six months in prison, received credit for good behavior and was released in 1995.

To Phillips, Smith’s story of struggle and redemption seemed genuine. During his ministry, Phillips said he’s seen other prisoners’ lives changed.

“I gave him some grace,” he said. “It was through the experience of incarceration that gave him the impetus to do this kind of a ministry. … It didn’t appear or feel contrived. It felt organic to me.”

The church initially agreed to allow the college to use some second story classroom space, but Phillips soon began asking questions about the college’s funding sources and business model.

At one point, he said Smith told him he was going to sell a second car to help pay the bills.

“As far as I can tell, he’s never really had a whole lot of financial support,” Phillips said. “He’s had no big contributors.”

When Smith hired Katie Parton as the college’s director of administration in September, Phillips grew even more concerned.

“My flag went up,” he said. “He didn’t have the money to hire her part time. … Two weeks later, he started hiring other people.”

Parton, who no longer works for the school, said her first contact with Smith came when she responded to a Craigslist ad seeking faculty for the college.

Parton earned her master’s in history from the University of Nebraska at Kearney earlier this year. After saddling herself with student loans that will likely take decades to pay off, Parton said she was drawn to Myrtle Beach Christian College’s commitment to tuition-free higher education.

“We have a whole lot of debt from these schools,” she said during an interview before the college left the church. “I can’t justify going to Coastal or USC or wherever and teaching and doing that to somebody else.”

Dawn Lazetera, the college’s then-director of organizational development, sat in on the initial interview with Parton. She, too, was attracted to Smith’s vision.

“I just really believe in the mission,” she said at that time. “A lot of people, they don’t go to school because of the cost or different reasons or their background. … Even with the prisoners, a lot of them are just a product of their environment. It’s not really their fault and they just need a helping hand sometimes.”

The interview proved to be a turning point for the college.

As the conversation was wrapping up, Parton excused herself and walked out of the room. When she returned, she asked that the story and photos not be published. Parton insisted that there was miscommunication between the church and the college and the church didn’t approve of any media coverage.

Phillips said he objected to the publicity because he had become concerned about the college’s viability and didn’t want people thinking of the church and the college as the same entity.

He also said the college needed more space than the church could provide.

After concluding that the program didn’t have the financial support to survive, Phillips informed its leaders that they couldn’t stay.

“Everything has basically moved back to Pennsylvania until they can secure more funds,” he said.

Last week, both Parton and Lazetera confirmed that they no longer work for the college. They wouldn’t say much beyond that.

“It’s just turned into a kind of messy situation,” Parton said. “It was like this domino effect.”

Bill Felder, a local counselor who was scheduled to teach two courses at the school, said he is not teaching there.

“I’m not going to be doing that,” he said and declined to comment further. Phillips, who was also scheduled to teach a class, said that won’t happen, either.

Smith responds

During phone interviews last week, Smith cited various reasons for the college’s troubles.

He said the board needed to hire a fundraiser, the demands of the prison ministry had become overwhelming and, as Phillips said, there wasn’t enough space at First United Methodist for the college’s operations.

Smith started out expressing gratitude for the church’s early assistance, but he later delved into criticism.

“There’s always this tendency, I think, on the part of some churches to sort of think of the building as our building,” he said. “And, ‘What are all those other people coming in here for?’ That’s a sad commentary when it gets to that point because it’s how churches die.”

Smith said he’s been planning a Grand Strand campus for years.
