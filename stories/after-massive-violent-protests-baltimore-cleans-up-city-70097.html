After Massive, Violent Protests Baltimore Cleans Up City

April 26, 2015 Black

© Algerina Perna/Baltimore Sun/TNS Sarita Mills, 34, stands across the street from Vaughn Greene Funeral Home, where a wake was held for Freddie Gray, on Sunday, April 26, 2015, in Baltimore.

Glass shards glinted in the Sunday morning sun on the sidewalk outside the 7-Eleven in Baltimore’s Inner Harbor.

“We are closed today 4/26/15. Sorry for the inconvenience,” said a makeshift sign taped to cardboard on the door where the glass had been shattered.

Above the door was a softball-size hole in the glass storefront.

It was a scene at a number of downtown businesses Sunday — recovering from the damage incurred during Saturday’s night’s demonstrations, held in protest of death of Freddie Gray. The 25-year-old died April 19, a week after sustaining a spinal cord injury while in police custody.

The demonstrations turned violent Saturday, with several arrests made, police vehicles damaged and storefronts vandalized.

Inspecting the damage at the 7-Eleven, a worker for the Downtown Partnership of Baltimore said the smashed glass struck him as oddly random.

It looked like the path of a tornado — one store hit but the surrounding ones, including a Dunkin’ Donuts and Royal Farms convenience store, left untouched.

The worker declined to be named because he said he was not authorized to speak publicly. Inside the 7-Eleven, a manager declined comment.

In the lobby of the Renaissance Baltimore Harborplace hotel, Tim and Jenny Rossback, visiting from Philadelphia, described the unusual feeling of approaching Camden Yards for Saturday night’s Orioles-Red Sox game and seeing rows of police officers.

“The anxiety-provoking part was when the police were retreating,” Tim Rossback said. “You could feel the tension in the air.”

The Rossbacks left the game early when it began to rain and saw damaged police cars being towed away.

They are sports fans and had come to town exclusively for the game. They said they would not be dissuaded from returning.

“This is not the only place this is happening,” Jenny Rossback said.
