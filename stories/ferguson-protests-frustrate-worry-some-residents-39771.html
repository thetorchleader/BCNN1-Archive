Ferguson Protests Frustrate, Worry Some Residents

October 15, 2014 National

Demonstrators confront police outside the Ferguson police station in Ferguson, Mo.
(Photo: Scott Olson, Getty Images)

Beauty salon owner ​Linda Hensiek cares deeply about the fate of this small Midwest city where she spent most of her 64 years, but she is also weary and worried.

Two blocks away from her hair salon, residents and out-of-towners have filled the street outside the Ferguson Police Department with angry protests to demand answers from their government about the Aug. 9 shooting death of Michael Brown, an unarmed 18-year-old black man killed by a white police officer.

The daily demonstrations have upset the city’s quiet rhythm with the influx of state police, agitators and hordes of journalists, Hensiek says. She and others in the city fear the daily demonstrations could cause lasting harm to Ferguson’s businesses and scare residents away from their longtime homes.

“They are destroying Ferguson,” Hensiek said. “Protesting to get the message out there is one thing. But the protesting that is done in our face to make us uncomfortable so that our businesses don’t flourish and our businesses have to close down and our community events get closed down is punishing us.”

Since Ferguson Officer Darren Wilson shot and killed Brown, thousands of protesters have called for his arrest. Police say Brown struggled with Wilson inside his police car and reached for the officer’s weapon. Brown’s family says the teen was killed as he raised his hands to surrender to the officer. A grand jury is hearing the case and will decide whether to charge Wilson with Brown’s death.

While civil unrest catapulted Ferguson into the national discourse on police brutality and racial profiling, Hensiek, who is white, sees Ferguson’s homier side — the friendly city where she learned to do hair, volunteered with Girl Scouts, worked with students as a teacher’s aid, and became friends with people from various races.

She now fears the city will forever be seen as racially divided, and worries about how the protesters might react to a grand jury decision that doesn’t result in Wilson’s indictment. Hensiek is one of many residents who expressed concern about the possibility of violence after the grand jury announcement. Mayor James Knowles III said authorities are working on emergency response plans.

“We have no idea what’s to come,” Hensiek said. “The unknown is absolutely frightening.”

Resident Charlotte Harral, 34, supported the protests at the beginning, but now wonders if they’ve gone on too long and lost their focus. Harral has opted out of the protests, staying at home instead with her 15-year-old son, Darrian, and 9-year-old daughter, Ramaiyan. She is most concerned that the protests might disrupt her children’s schooling.

“I do want justice for Mike Brown but now a part of me thinks the protesting is kind of petty,” Harral, an Army Reserves mechanic, said. “Now there is no meaning behind the protesting. You are just doing it just to do it. It’s like you are using a slain young man’s name to give a reason for negativity.”

Harral, who is black, said she hasn’t experienced racism and thinks many of the demonstrators are resorting to racial stereotypes of both black and white people — “categorizing all white people for one white guy’s mistake,” she says.

After another white police officer shot and killed Vonderrit Myers, an 18-year-old black man in St. Louis, the protests spread out of Ferguson and into Donald Stepps’ St. Louis neighborhood.

“I’m really just sick of it,” Stepps, a waiter, said as demonstrators chanted nearby. “I can’t sleep. I was just lying in bed and I could hear a helicopter. It sounded like it was right over the house.”

Stepps, 27, who is black, speculated that the protesters “have no jobs and nowhere to be.”

“That’s why they are out here protesting day and night,” he said.
