Keli Goff says In the 2014 Midterm Elections, We Should Look for More Diversity Among the Ranks of Democrats’ Political Operatives

July 31, 2014 Black

During a press call Tuesday, Rep. Steve Israel of New York, chairman of the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee, laid out what he sees as the Democrats’ best chance at victory in the upcoming midterm elections.

Unlike previous election cycles, when much of the party’s outreach focused almost exclusively on mobilizing key groups to go to the polls—particularly minority voters—this year Democrats are employing a new tactic: recruiting and mobilizing more minority staff.

Even though black voters are the party’s most reliable constituency, a long-standing complaint about Democratic campaigns has been that candidates only show up in the black community a couple of months before Election Day, and when they do, rarely do they have any black decision-makers from the campaigns in tow, because rarely do the campaigns have any.

To this day, only two African Americans have managed a major-party presidential campaign: Donna Brazile managed former Vice President Al Gore’s 2000 presidential campaign, while Maggie Williams briefly managed Hillary Clinton’s 2008 race. Williams replaced Patti Solis Doyle, the first and only Latina to manage a major presidential campaign. Then-Sen. John Kerry’s 2004 campaign was heavily criticized for a lack of staff diversity. And recently, Democrats faced criticism for their dismal record in hiring minority-owned consulting firms.

Asked by The Root if the criticism has fueled this newfound commitment to staffing diversity, Israel gave a candid response. “It’s about accountability and winnability,” he said.

“So when we see an audit [about lack of diversity in consultants], we want to be accountable,” Israel continued. “But many of the decisions we made, for instance—hiring a diversity director, hiring a national training director, launching the vendor Internet database [which tracks diversity]—were decisions we made prior to the audit [of consultant diversity].”

Of the second issue, winnability, he commented, “As I said, sometimes you have to be cold-blooded, clear-eyed and diagnostic to win elections, and my cold-blooded, clear-eyed, diagnostic elements tell me that drop-off is going to be a big issue in this campaign. We have to do something about it, and we have to do a better job communicating with African-American voters and mobilizing them, and that it is integrated into our new field programs, tactics and strategies.”

Asked whether the DCCC has found a way to address one of the greatest barriers to diversity in politics, it becomes clear that it, like many institutions, still has work to do. While the organization has a number of programs aimed at recruiting young people of color into the field of politics—including its Organizing Fellowship program, which has recruited hundreds of young people, many from HBCUs, and helped them find employment on campaigns—it still does not offer paid internships.
