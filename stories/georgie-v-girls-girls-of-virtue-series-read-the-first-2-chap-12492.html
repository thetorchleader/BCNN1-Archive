“Georgie” (V-Girls: Girls of Virtue Series): Read the First 2 Chapters Free

May 18, 2013 Uncategorized

by Danita Whyte
with Danaé, Daniqua, and Danyelle Whyte

Hi friends,

My name is Georgianna Delrose but you can just call me Georgie –
all my friends do. I live in London, England (not far from the famed
Abbey Road of the Beatles), which tourists crowd regularly. It’s really
nice here and rains a lot even in the summer, but I like it. I live
with my father and mum and our three Siamese cats – Flo, Spree,
and Charlie. I used to be the only child, until I got a little sister.

My little sister came two years ago – when the most important
thing in my life happened. You see, I go to church with my parents all
the time, and take part in all the youth events we have, and two years
ago, I thought attending church made me a Christian. But when I was
fourteen, I took a mission trip to Cambodia and on that trip I
discovered a lot about God, myself, and my relationship with Him. I’m
sixteen now, and life couldn’t be better, but to understand why I’m
saying that – you have to read my story.

Georgie

Chapter 1

Georgie shut the front door of her house behind her and ran down the
porch steps to join her parents who were waiting in their car. It was a
calm spring evening in March and they were on their way to Bible study
at church. The smell of flowers hung heavy in the air and the last rays
from the day’s sun gave off a golden light as the family left their
driveway and started off down the road.

Georgie was fourteen years old and had auburn brown hair with matching
eyes. She was the only child of Mr. and Mrs. Delrose, however, they had
three cats. Mr. and Mrs. Delrose both worked at their family bakery
located on the first floor of their Victorian style home. Mr. and Mrs.
Delrose named their bakery ‘Sweet Things by Spree’ after Georgie’s
second cat who greeted every customer who came through the door. A
picture of Spree was on every bag that went out, and as long as they
were not allergic to cats, Georgie allowed the customers to take photos
with them. Because of her parent’s business, the family’s living
quarters were all on the second floor and that’s where Georgie stayed
most of the time when she was out of boarding school. Sometimes,
however, she would help her father make scones and help serve the
customers tea who stayed to dine in their bakery. The Delrose house
always smelled of spices and other good things.

On this spring evening, Georgie was especially excited because a
missionary from China was coming to speak to their youth group and she
couldn’t wait to hear of all the adventures she was sure he would tell.
She also couldn’t wait to see her best friend, Cate Le Feuvre, who had
been out all month with the flu. She had bought a special gift to give
to her at the youth meeting.

“Are you ready to start school again in three weeks?” her
father asked from the driver’s side as they sped down the main road.

Georgie frowned. “Not really,” she answered. “Our
head master, Mrs. Peroic, is so difficult and I really was wishing to
have learned the piano by now–“

“Have you learned the guitar well?” her mother interrupted.

“Yes, quite well,” Mr. Delrose answered. “Thanks to
me.”

Georgie grinned. “Yes, thank you. You were a delightful teacher
but I still really, really want to learn the piano, and besides, I
think it would be nice if we all took a trip away from here before
school begins again. We could always leave the bakery with the Davids.
Couldn’t we? What do you think, Father?”

“I don’t think it’s a bad idea at all,” Mr. Delrose
answered. The Davids family were Georgie’s next door neighbors,
consisting of five boys, three aunts, one uncle, and the mother and
father. Sometimes the three aunts would come over and help Mrs. Delrose
bake. They were quite  a party with their messy hair-dos and
outlandish dresses, but always a lot of fun to be with.

“But we are planning a trip,” Mrs. Delrose reminded her
daughter and husband. “In the summer we are going to
Scotland.”

“Again? We always go to Scotland, Mum,” Georgie replied.

“Quite true,” Mr. Delrose answered. “Perhaps we
should consider Scandinavia.”

Georgie gave a thumbs-up as they pulled into the church parking space.
“We could pretend to be Vikings,” she said. Her parents
laughed as she jumped out of the car and dashed away to the gymnasium
where the youth met to have worship. “Bye, see you soon!”
she called.

Once inside the gym, Georgie looked around for her friend, Cate. She
soon spotted her among a group of other kids who were helping to set up
music equipment on the stage and went to join them. “Hi,
Cate,” she greeted. “I’m so happy you got better! You look
great. Did you get my cards I sent you?”

Cate had light hair and blue eyes with deep dimples that seemed to only
get deeper each time she smiled. She was slightly shorter than most
girls her age, but she had a big heart that made up for it. Cate gave
Georgie a hug. “Yes,” she assured her. “I got all
your cards. Thank you so much.”

“Guess what I brought you?” Georgie asked her. “It’s
one of your favorites.”

“Blueberry cupcakes with cinnamon,” Cate answered correctly
as Georgie handed the package to her. “I can’t wait to eat
them.”

Just then the stage lights came on and Cate and Georgie went to take
their seats with the other kids on the lighted disco floor. Everyone
clapped as the youth band came out to start their worship off with
upbeat praise songs. The girls waved to their friend, Marshall Lewis,
who was playing that night on the guitar.

Usually, it was Georgie who played the guitar for their church youth
band and she always had a blast. Marshall had asked her to teach him
how to play and after he finished all his lessons she had decided to
give him a chance to show off his skills in front of their group, which
Mr. Dez Hersheys, their youth leader, had agreed she could do. Georgie
could tell Marshall was excited. He grinned from his place on the stage.

The music director, Blaire Tristan, grabbed her microphone. “Hey
guys!” she called out to the excited crowd. “Are you ready
to praise God?”

Georgie and the rest of the kids cheered. Blaire grinned. “All
right Rebels, let’s go!”

Rebels…with a Mission was the official name of Georgie’s youth group.
Dez Hersheys had given them that name because he emphasized as their
leader that he wanted them to rebel against and buck the world while
fully embracing and following Jesus Christ. The kids around the gym
jumped and sang along to the upbeat music.

After a few more songs, Dez Hersheys came up to the stage. The kids
applauded him loudly. Dez, who was born in Africa, was in England to
finish college. He wore chain necklaces and lots of designer belts. The
Rebels youth group thought they had the most engaging person to be
their leader because he was always upbeat and full of excitement. Cate
thought that he must live on coffee to be so filled with energy all of
the time.

“Hi ya,” he greeted the kids. “So glad you all could
make it here tonight, because I’ve got a special guest for you. Lately,
over the past few weeks, we’ve been putting a special emphasis on
missions because what are we called to do?”

“Carry out the Great Commission,” the kids answered him.

Dez nodded. “Right,” he told them. “So all the way
from China, I want you to give Jiayi and Biyu Chenguang a real brill
welcome. These guys have come all the way from Asia to talk to us about
mission work, their mission work, and how we can share Jesus right
where we are and around the world.” Dez joined the kids on the
floor as Jiayi and his wife, Biyu Chenguang, took the stage. They both
wore jeans and had dark hair. Biyu had light red highlights in her
hair. Jiayi and Biyu had been missionaries in China for five years and
were on a short vacation break in London to raise more funds before
they went back. The kids clapped for them.

Using slides and interactive maps and photos, Jiayi told the Rebels
about the work with the Chinese people. He told them that although it
wasn’t as bad as it once was, mission work in China still remained
challenging. “There are more Christians in China now than ever
before,” Jiayi informed them. “However, the Chinese
government is still largely communist and many Chinese Christians face
persecution daily. Many of them are not allowed to worship God freely
like we are able to do here in London. However, we must not let that
discourage us. Christians in China are quite strong in their faith. One
of the Bible passages that I really like to remind the churches there
of is found in Romans 8:35-39:

Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? shall tribulation, or
distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?
As it is written, For thy sake we are killed all the day long; we are
accounted as sheep for the slaughter. Nay, in all these things we are
more than conquerors through him that loved us. For I am persuaded,
that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor
powers, nor things present, nor things to come, Nor height, nor depth,
nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of
God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.

Jiayi continued, “I am quite sure that none of these things will
cause the Christians in China to forsake Jesus.”

After Jiayi finished, Biyu Chenguang showed them a video of young
people their age in China who each had different stories of how they
came to know Christ and how excited they were about witnessing to
others in their country. Georgie felt happy for them.

Dez placed his arms around Jiayi and Biyu before they left the stage.
“These guys are really cool,” he told the kids. “And
you are going to get the chance to hang out with them in Cambodia…of
all places. In seven days, Jiayi and Biyu are leaving to go to Asia
again, to the kingdom of Cambodia, and our Rebel youth group has been
invited to go along. I’ve got slips for everyone to give to their
parents to sign for permission for you to go. How stoked is everyone
now?”

“Really, really stoked!” Cate exclaimed as she emerged with
Georgie from the gym, waving her paper slip around.

“I hope my parents let me go,” Georgie said. “I think
they will.” Marshall came running up with his guitar case.
“My parents have already signed mine,” he told them.
“So I get to go to Cambodia with Jiayi and Biyu and Dez. Dez is
coming with us too. Those missionaries were so inspiring.”

“And so were you,” Georgie complimented him. “Your
guitar playing was rocking tonight.”

Marshall grinned, “Really? Thanks. Maybe we’ll get to play
together in Cambodia.”

“That would be nice. We should start a band?” Cate
suggested.

“What shall we call ourselves?” Georgie wondered
thoughtfully. “How about the Rebels?”

“I like it,” Marshall laughed. “See you later.”
He went off to join his parents who were waiting for him by their car.
Georgie and Cate said goodbye and went off to find their parents who
were coming out of the main church building with the other adults.

Georgie talked excitedly about the Chinese missionaries and their trip
to Cambodia all the way back home and asked Mr. and Mrs. Delrose could
they sign her paper slip.

Her mother smiled as she took the paper Georgie handed her.
“Well, I guess you’ll be going on a spring trip after all,”
she told her.

Chapter 2

Seven days later, Georgie was sitting on the porch swing beside the
front door of her house. It was still quite early and her parents had
just opened their bakery. She pulled her socks up and checked one more
time in her blue duffle bags to make sure she had packed everything she
needed for her trip to Cambodia including hiking boots, her Bible, and
her cell phone that had a camera to send photos back to her parents.
Dez had told them that they would be gone for two weeks and if they had
the time, would be able to do some sightseeing for fun.

Taking out her father’s goodbye present — a  Bakewell tart
with raspberry jam and almonds –Georgie looked down her quiet
street to see if the church bus was coming before sitting back down to
eat. She could hardly wait to be on the plane and off to Cambodia.

A slight ruffle in the bushes beside her house caused her to look up.
The youngest Davids boy, Aaron, was climbing up on their porch. Georgie
frowned, “What do you want?” she asked him. A Davids boy
coming over this early could never mean anything good.

Aaron glanced at the tart in her hand and sat on her bags.
“Nothing,” he answered. “Where are you going? Are you
going to collect turtles?”

“Of course not,” Georgie replied.

“Well, I wish you were,” he continued. “Then you
could take me with you because Wesley, Will, Winston, and Wylie are all
playing knights and won’t let me in at their round table because I
don’t have a ‘W’ name. Why don’t I have a ‘W’ name?”

“Because an ‘A’ name is better,” Georgie said, trying to
cheer him. “If you go inside, Mum might give you a tart, since
you’re our first customer today. Do you have any money?”

Aaron stuck his hand in his pocket and came out with a penny. “Is
this enough?” Georgie shook her head no.

Aaron dug some more in his pockets. “How about this?” he
asked pulling out a slimy gray worm. “It’s dead because I sat on
it, but I’m sure it’ll make enough.”

Georgie was relieved to see the bus coming down the road. “Clear
off,” she ordered Aaron, dropping the rest of her tart into his
hands. She poked her face into the open front window and kissed her
cats who were lying lazily on the sill. “Bye, Mum; bye,
Father,” she called to them. “I’ll see you in two weeks and
tell you all about Cambodia.”

Her mother blew a kiss.

“Stay safe,” her dad answered. “Cherrio!”

Georgie picked up her guitar case and duffle bags and boarded the bus
as it pulled to a stop in front of her house. She cheerfully said,
“Good morning” to the bus driver as she proceeded to find a
seat.

“Hi ya,” Dez greeted Georgie. “Quite a lot of stuff
you’re bringing. One thing you should know about mission trips,
missionaries travel light.” Georgie laughed and went to join Cate
who was sitting towards the back. “Isn’t that a Davids boy on
your porch?” she asked.

“Yes,” Georgie answered. “And he took my
breakfast.”

Marshall was sitting in a middle seat with Jiayi and Biyu Chenguang.
“Did you bring your guitar?” he called back to Georgie.

“Sure, I did,” she answered. “What about you?”

“Yeah, and Jiayi has an electric keytor. It’s really brill.”

Besides Marshall, Georgie, and Cate, fourteen other kids had signed up
for the mission trip to Cambodia. There were two sets of twins: Elie
and Diana Bradshaw, who could talk endlessly it seemed. Georgie found
herself wishing that they hadn’t come along because the twins weren’t
particularly nice to her. They were always teasing her about wearing
long, striped socks. Elie and Diana had even more luggage than Georgie
and were full of giggles about taking this trip. This was the twins’
first time out of London. Georgie looked at one of their heavy bags and
imagined what was in it: pink nail polish, pink hair ribbons, and pink
slippers.

“Fashionistas rarely make good missionaries,” Dez had joked.

David and John Lerwick were brothers, but were two years apart and both
had rings of freckles across their noses and cheeks. Sometimes David
and John would come over and play with the Davids boys while giving
Georgie a headache with all the noise they made and bugs they produced.
Away from the Davids, however, they could be quite nice. Andrew Dupree
and Bethany Dupree were the only brother and sister to come along.
Andrew was always reading, so it made sense that he was the smartest in
their group and would sometimes assist Dez with their Bible lessons.
His sister liked to hang around with Georgie and Cate and often led
their group in prayer.

May-Kelsey Bairds was as sweet as sugar and was the youngest of the
Rebels, at twelve years old. She was very creative and led their group
whenever they had to put on a dance for the church. Georgie loved to
hear her stories about dancing bears and talking birds.

Adam Cameron, at seventeen, was the oldest of the Rebels. This would be
his last year as a Rebel, so he was really looking forward to this
Cambodian trip. The rest of the kids were going to miss him when he
left the youth group.

Eddie Westhorn and Parker Hill had earned the most stars in Bible
memorization. They were best friends and also gigantic fans of soccer.
They bought tickets for the entire youth group whenever the Three Lions
were playing a major match.

Kala Pipers helped Blaire Tristan write some of their songs and
oftentimes sang back up. William Rooney, who was the same age as
Georgie, also helped Blaire sing and would sometimes play the drums.

The last two girls who came along, Natalie Minsters and Anna Morton,
went to the same boarding school as Georgie. They were less fashion
conscious than the twins, but liked to wear lacy skirts and feathers in
their hair. They had been appointed by Dez to head their outreach
activities and often led the Rebels on expeditions to give toys to
London orphans.

Finally, there were the five adults who were taking the trip with the
kids to Cambodia. Among them were: Dez Hersheys, Blaire Tristan, who
hoped the Cambodians loved music; Biyu Chenguang and Jiayi Chenguang;
and Bart Brown, the appointed bus driver.

Andrew, who was sitting in the seat in front of Georgie and Cate,
pulled his nose out of the book that was lying open in his lap.
“Hey Dez,” he called up to the front, “I’m reading an
encyclopedia on the kingdom of Cambodia. It says that their official
language is Khmer. How are they going to understand us?”

“Jiayi and Biyu can speak Khmer and Blaire and I have learned a
few phrases,” Dez answered him. “So whatever we don’t
understand, they can translate.”

“What else does your encyclopedia say?” Marshall asked.
“I did searches on the internet. It said Cambodia has a
king.”

“Ooh,” Elie said. “Will we get to meet him?”

“I don’t think so,” Blaire answered.

“Does Cam have beauty parlors?” Diana asked.

“I certainly hope not,” Georgie replied. “And I don’t
think countries have nicknames.”

Andrew flipped through some more pages in his encyclopedia. “It
says here that Cambodia has many national parks. Some of the animals of
Cambodia include elephants, snakes, and tigers. Cambodia also has a
national museum and its capital is called Phnom Penh.”

Elie laughed, “That’s a funny name.”

“Will we be staying in Phnom Penh?” Adam asked Jiayi.

“No, but we will get to visit there for sightseeing,” Jiayi
answered. “We are going to stay in a village close to Phnom
Penh.”

Bethany took her brother’s book. “Most of Cambodia is
Buddhist,” she pointed out. “In fact, only two percent of
the people are Christians.”

“How sad,” Cate commented.

“Yeah,” Dez agreed. “Which means that there are a lot
of people who haven’t heard that God loves them and we’re going there
to tell them.”

“Won’t we get in trouble?” May-Kelsey wanted to know.
“I thought Christians got killed or put in jail for loving Jesus
over there.”

“Well, some Cambodians who have turned to Christ have been
accused of being betrayers,” Biyu Chenguang answered. “Now,
it’s not so severe. Today, there are over two thousand Christian
churches and more Christians in Cambodia than ever before.”

“And that’s encouraging,” Jiayi agreed. “There used
to be only two hundred Christians alive in Cambodia at one time.”

“So Christians aren’t being hurt anymore?” Eddie Westhorn
asked.

“Not like they were back then,” Dez assured the kids.
“A really bad guy name Pol Pot used to rule Cambodia, and when he
ruled, a lot of Christians were put to death. During that time, the
Christians nearly became invisible, but now they are back, even though
some still live in fear. However, we’re not going to go to Cambodia in
fear because God has not given us the spirit of fear. What’s that
verse, Eddie and Parker, my Bible stars?”

“For the Spirit God gave us does not make us timid, but gives us
power, love, and self-discipline,” Parker said.

Eddie finished for him, “So do not be ashamed of the testimony
about our Lord or of me his prisoner. Rather, join with me in suffering
for the gospel, by the power of God.”

“Right. So we aren’t afraid,” Dez said. “And anyway,
what do we call ourselves?”

“Rebels!” Natalie Minsters said.

“That wasn’t loud enough.”

“Rebels!” the kids yelled in unison.

“Yeah and rebels aren’t afraid,” Blaire replied.
