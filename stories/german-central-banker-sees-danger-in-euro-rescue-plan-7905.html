German Central Banker Sees Danger in Euro Rescue Plan

August 26, 2012 Uncategorized

The president of the German central bank said in an interview published Sunday that he remained staunchly opposed to government bond purchases by the European Central Bank, a position that could make it more difficult to deploy a weapon many economists believe is essential to saving the euro.

Jens Weidmann, the president of the Bundesbank, Germany’s central bank, said in an interview with the newsmagazine Der Spiegel that such bond purchases would be equivalent to printing money to finance governments and that he was against them.
“We should not underestimate the danger,” he said. “Central bank financing can be as addictive as a drug.”
Mr. Weidmann has often expressed opposition to bond purchases, but the timing of his forcefully worded statement on the issue is significant. It comes a little more than a week before the E.C.B. governing council is expected to provide details of how it would carry out a commitment to intervene in bond markets to contain borrowing costs for euro zone countries like Spain and Italy.
Mario Draghi, the president of the E.C.B., may be reluctant to alienate Mr. Weidmann, whose views are in tune with a broad segment of the German public and political class. Any intervention in bond markets could be less effective if investors doubt the E.C.B.’s resolve.
Mr. Weidmann’s statement also comes amid an intense debate in Germany about how much risk its taxpayers are willing to assume to save the common currency. Mr. Weidmann said in the interview that it would be a mistake for the E.C.B. “to guarantee that every member remains in the euro zone no matter what the price.”
Antonis Samaras, the prime minister of Greece, just ended a visit to Berlin and Paris to plead for more time to overhaul the Greek economy. Angela Merkel, the German chancellor, received Mr. Samaras cordially on Friday and said she was committed to keeping Greece in the euro zone, while insisting that Greece should stick to conditions set by international lenders. François Hollande, the French president, said Saturday after a meeting with Mr. Samaras that, while Greece should press forward with reforms, they should be “tolerable for the population.”
But while Ms. Merkel has praised Mr. Draghi and taken a less strident tone toward Greece recently, many Germans and even many in her own governing coalition continue to blame Greeks for the crisis and remain hostile to easing the terms for aid. Mr. Weidmann’s statements could embolden that camp.
