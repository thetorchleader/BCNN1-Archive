Kwame Kilpatrick Trial: Three African-Americans Now in Jury Pool

September 11, 2012 Uncategorized

Jury selection in Kwame Kilpatrick’s trial ended on a secretive note Monday when an African-American woman was questioned privately about a sensitive issue and was then quickly placed in the pool with no objection.
The sidebar came after a prosecutor asked her whether she believed people were sometimes wrongfully convicted. She said yes.
The judge called everyone to the bench. Minutes later, the woman was placed in the jury pool. None of the defense lawyers openly questioned the woman, a nurse who runs marathons.
The jury pool is now up to 24 people, including three African Americans who advanced into the pool Monday. The others are a stay-at-home dad and a medical assistant who expressed concerns about losing her health benefits and job if she is a juror in the trial, which is expected to last four months. U.S. District Judge Nancy Edmunds is waiting to hear whether the woman’s employer can accommodate the prospective juror.
Race has been an issue in the trial, with defense lawyers raising concerns about a lack of African Americans in the jury pool in the Eastern District of Michigan. Black people account for 21% of the entire district, which means the final panel of 12 jurors and six alternates should include at least three African Americans.
With 24 people in the pool now, the lawyers and judge have 42 left to get to their goal of 66, from which the final panel will be picked.
Kwame Kilpatrick; his father, Bernard Kilpatrick; contractor and longtime Kwame Kilpatrick friend Bobby Ferguson, and ex-water boss Victor Mercado are charged with running a criminal enterprise out of the mayor’s office to enrich themselves.
Among those who advanced to the next round is a former Marine who just became a deacon at a predominantly black church. The man said he knew about Kilpatrick’s past legal troubles, including the affair and perjury scandal that drove him from office, but said he wouldn’t hold it against the former mayor. He also had some positive things to say about Kilpatrick, followed by a stinging comment.
“I think he’s charismatic and intelligent, and he let this city down,” the prospective juror said. “I think he’d still be mayor if he didn’t get in trouble.”
Unlike in most trials, the jurors’ names are being withheld to protect their privacy. Extra precautions also are being taken to prevent reporters from finding out details about their lives. For example, when prospective jurors are asked about something deemed sensitive, the judge and lawyers question them privately.
