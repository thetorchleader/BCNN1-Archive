Missouri’s Bonhomme Presbyterian Church Fights to Hold on to Property After Split With Denomination Over Homosexuality, Abortion

February 2, 2015 Christian

Exterior view of Bonhomme Presbyterian Church in Chesterfield, as photographed at sunset Wednesday, Jan. 28, 2015. Photo by Sid Hastings

The second-oldest Presbyterian church west of the Mississippi River, Bonhomme Presbyterian, is struggling to hang on to its historical roots — and its lavish Chesterfield property.

The problem stems from the church’s recent decision to break from its former denomination — the Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.).

Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.) is the largest Presbyterian denomination in the country, with about 1.8 million members. The denomination supports abortion rights. In 2011, it lifted its longtime ban on gay clergy, and its stance against same-sex marriage is under debate.

Bonhomme Presbyterian Church disagrees with the denomination’s views on these and other issues.

The Rev. Tom Pfizenmaier, senior pastor of the church, calls Bonhomme a “theological outlier.”

Rather than continue to have disagreements, Bonhomme has opted to join a new, more conservative denomination: Covenant Order of Evangelical Presbyterians, sometimes referred to as ECO, which has grown rapidly since its launch in 2012. Currently, 177 congregations and 270 pastors belong to the denomination.

Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.) leaders, however, argue that whatever theological direction the congregation chooses, the church property remains theirs.

“The Presbyterian church is becoming more and more hostile toward churches that are trying to leave,” Pfizenmaier said one recent afternoon in his office.

When asked about the case, Anita Hendrix, leader of the Giddings-Lovejoy Presbytery, the denomination’s governing body, which has jurisdiction over Bonhomme and other churches in the region, said “the whole process here is very painful.”

“We’re grieving for the loss of our sister church, praying for all the members and pastors there,” she said. “It’s a loss for us and the whole church.”

It’s a debate that’s been repeated across the country in recent years as more and more churches have decided to leave the denomination because of theological differences. In 2013 alone, Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.) lost 224 churches, at least 148 of which joined other denominations.

Churches that have severed their relationship with the denomination have inadvertently pushed questions of property ownership to the forefront.

The Archdiocese of St. Louis engaged in a similar struggle in 2003, when it asked St. Stanislaus Kostka Polish Catholic Parish to hand over control of its property and assets.

Years later the archdiocese sued for control of the church. In 2012, a St. Louis Circuit Court ruled in favor of the parish.

Last year, members of one of the largest congregations in Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.), Menlo Park Presbyterian in the San Francisco Bay Area, voted to break from the denomination, despite being ordered to pay $8.89 million to keep its property.

“I would say Giddings-Lovejoy Presbytery is a very good microcosm of what’s going on in the national church,” Pfizenmaier, 60, said.

“At the end of the day, if we lose the property, we lose the property,” Pfizenmaier said.

“We don’t think that’s going to happen, but if it does, then we’ll find our way forward. The church is not the property. It’s the people.”
