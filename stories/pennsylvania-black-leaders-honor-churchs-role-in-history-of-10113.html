Pennsylvania Black Leaders Honor Church's Role In History of Emancipation Proclamation

January 16, 2013 Uncategorized

Lenwood Sloan looked out at the crowd at the Harrisburg Transportation Center and said, “Friends, we are standing on history today.”
The commanding brick, block-long building sits on the original location of Bethel Village A.M.E. Church. The church later moved to Short Street, and, there, black leaders discussed the Emancipation Proclamation.
Sloan, co-chairman of the Friends of the Bethel Trail, joined local leaders to commemorate the 150th anniversary of the forum of black leaders discussing the Emancipation Proclamation.
In addition, the event afforded the perfect opportunity to unveil the Bethel Trail — the “spine in an invisible body,” the development of the black community in Harrisburg, Sloan said.
The proclamation President Abraham Lincoln signed Jan. 1, 1863 freed the slaves in the Confederate states.
Local black leaders Jan. 15, 1863 discussed and debated the document at Bethel Village A.M.E. Church’s former location on Short Street.
The church originally was built in 1835 at the site of the transportation center at 415 Market St., said the Rev. Martin D. Odom, pastor of the church.
The black leaders ultimately passed a resolution endorsing the Emancipation Proclamation, said Odom. Two years later, the 13th Amendment abolished slavery.
“This is significant because it happened at a time when many blacks were ambivalent as to whether or not to participate in the Civil War,” Odom said, regarding the proclamation.
“And as a result of that meeting, many blacks in this Harrisburg area began to sign up for the Union Army and to fight in the Civil War.”
