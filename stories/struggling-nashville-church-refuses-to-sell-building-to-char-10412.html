Struggling Nashville Church Refuses to Sell Building to Charter School

January 26, 2013 Uncategorized

An East Nashville charter school’s plans to take over a neighborhood church — already approved by the city’s zoning appeals board and historic commission — hit a roadblock this month when church trustees voted down the deal.
Liberty Collegiate Academy’s offer of between $100,000 and $200,000 to buy Hobson United Methodist Church was unrealistic, its pastor says, given that the property and building appraised at $1.3 million, and neighboring home prices are soaring.
“The Liberty people said $100,000 was their floor,” the Rev. Sonnye Dixon said. “One of our church members said that was the crawl space.”
The deal’s collapse could be a problem for Liberty, because the school must vacate a charter incubator at 10th Street and Fatherland Avenue by July. After spending two years there, it’s time for Liberty to make way for the incubator’s next occupant, Nashville Classical, which opens in August.
Eastwood Neighbors residents already were celebrating Hobson’s switch from Methodist church to charter school. In Liberty’s first year, its students outperformed their Metro Nashville public school counterparts in every subject, making it a welcome addition.
“We will be discussing exciting plans for the conversion,” the community group’s blog chirps, announcing a December meeting.
According to Liberty board member Wood Caldwell, a principal with Southeast Venture, negotiations for Liberty to buy the building weren’t ever put in writing, and Liberty’s board didn’t have an appraisal in hand when those happened.
Instead, his group wanted a 25-year lease that would have paid Hobson $130,000 upfront, put $130,000 annual lease payments into building repairs for 15 years and then paid the congregation $130,000 annually for the final 10 years.
The church’s appeal, Caldwell said, was its size and history of public service.
Metro Councilman Peter Westerholm, who met with the church’s neighbors about its proposed transition into a charter school, was taken aback early Friday to learn that move hadn’t been approved.
“My understanding was that Liberty Collegiate had obtained approval to work with Hobson to take ownership of the building,” Westerholm said.
Not even close, Dixon said. The congregation needs a fair price now to move forward in a new location, he said, and at age 60 with retirement in view, he’d feel uncomfortable advising it to enter a 25-year lease.
His congregation left the building in December, and about 60 of the members meet in the old St. Andrews UMC building on Clarksville Pike in North Nashville.
Liberty serves 200 students in grades 5 and 6, and there are plans for it to expand a grade each year through 12th grade. Its budget this school year is $2.1 million, Caldwell said.
