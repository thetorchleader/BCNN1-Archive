Sea Lion Pup Rescued From San Francisco Streets

May 1, 2015 Science

A stranded sea lion pup lying next to a car on street in San Francisco's Marina District on April 30, 2015. Rescuers form the Marine Mammal Center netted the 11-month-old male and returned him to the rehabilitation facility in nearby Sausalito, just a month after he was released into the Pacific after being found malnourished and sick on a beach in central California. (PHOTO CREDIT: Adam Ratner, The Marine Mammal Center)

You never know what you’ll see on the streets of San Francisco.

At dawn Thursday, it was a stranded, starving sea lion pup named “Rubbish,” who was spotted crossing a wide boulevard beside the Golden Gate just a month after being nursed to health and released back into the Pacific.

An hour later, rescuers from the Marine Mammal Center, aided by city police, got the young male out from under a parked car across from the Marina Green and the Small Craft Harbor. After netting him, they coaxed the pup into a carrier for his extended round trip to the rehabilitation facility in the Marin Headlands that cared for him several weeks ago.

Rubbish is among the more than 1,800 sea lion pups that have been found stranded along the California coast in just the first four months of the year — a record following two years of above-normal strandings.

Since the first of the year, the center, in Sausalito, said it has responded to strandings of 927 California sea lions, mostly young pups. That’s more than for all of 2014. The veterinary staff and a cadre of volunteers have rehabbed and released 159 animals and continuing to care for 122 sea lions. The rest — nearly 650 — died or were so sick they were euthanized.

Scientists attribute the record number of strandings of starving and sick young pups to warmer ocean water brought on by a shift in the climate. That’s made it harder for nursing mothers to find food, causing them to abandon weaning pups as they travel farther for fish in colder waters.

Malnourished and suffering from pneumonia, Rubbish was initially rescued Feb. 8 by the Santa Barbara Marine Mammal Center and then transferred to the Marin facility, the world’s largest such rehabilitation facility.

When he arrived, he was eight months old and weighed only 30 pounds. By the time he was released March 23 at the Point Reyes National Seashore, he had beaten the infection and nearly doubled his weight.

By Thursday, after five weeks on his own in the wilds, he had lost 17 pounds.

“We don’t get a lot of restrands; normally fewer than 20 a year,” Shawn Johnson, director of veterinary science, told USA TODAY. “Most (rescues) figure out how to find the food they need to survive.”
