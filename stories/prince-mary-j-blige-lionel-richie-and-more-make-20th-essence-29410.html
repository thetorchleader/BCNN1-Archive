Prince, Mary J. Blige, Lionel Richie and More Make 20th Essence Festival All About Music

Date not recorded in the original archive Entertainment

(Photo: Gerald Herbert, AP)

In years before, Kanye West started his evening at Essence Festival on a scissor-lift in the middle of the Mercedes-Benz Superdome, and Beyoncé rolled out an ambitious, state-of-the-art show. Friday set the tone for the festival’s 20th year with little production as Prince, Nile Rodgers and Janelle Monáe made the night all about music, and Mary J. Blige, Jill Scott, Charlie Wilson and Lionel Richie followed suit.

THE PROVOCATEUR: Janelle Monáe sees herself as a provocateur and introduced one song saying: “They told me revolutionary music is out of style. Do you believe it? Because I don’t.” For 45 or so minutes Friday night, she worked to live up to that thought, right down to her wardrobe — a fashionable riff on Stanley Kubrick’s confrontational film A Clockwork Orange with a white blouse, white pants, black biker boots and suspenders.

The show polarized the audience, and those in the upper reaches of the dome would later complain on Facebook that they found it hard to connect with her energetic glam-rock funk. Even in the front, the crowd there for headliner Prince’s hits (he stepped out to play part of the closing guitar solo when Monáe covered his Let’s Go Crazy) was kind but reserved in its response to the new-to-them material, but a cover of James Brown’s I Got You (I Feel Good) got the audience’s attention. By the conclusion of the Brown-influenced Tightrope, Monáe’s best-known song, she had won over much of the room.

THESE ARE THE ‘GOOD TIMES’: Nile Rodgers, guitarist and founder of Chic, came out alone with a camera to take pictures of the crowd. “The last time I was in this building was with Luther Vandross,” he announced, then gave Essence the old-school R&B it loves, starting with Everybody Dance, the band’s disco hit from 1977. Rodgers is the only original member still with the group, and it quickly became apparent that bassist Jerry Barnes is very good, but he didn’t attempt to match the late Bernard Edwards’ signature, melodic bass lines. That didn’t diminish the cavalcade of hits, which included songs by Diana Ross and Sister Sledge that Rodgers and Edwards wrote and produced, as well Let’s Dance (with Prince joining in), a song Rodgers co-wrote and produced for David Bowie.

The set’s highlight came during the closing Good Times, when Rodgers invited 30 or so people onstage to dance. They took over, completely obscuring the band. Many did the Electric Slide on the front of the stage, and a Soul Train line of sorts broke out as women strutted out of the pack one by one for spotlight moments. Off to one side, two waifs in American flag dresses shot selfies in front of the band.

PRINCE, THE PEOPLE-PLEASER: Prince, being Prince, began enigmatically with a screensaver-like projection of a fish in a tank on a curtain in front of the stage. “We’re going to play 14 hits in a row,” he announced as the curtain was drawn to reveal him and 3rdEyeGirl, the all-female band that now backs him. He was true to his word, starting with Let’s Go Crazy rearranged as a grinding blues-rock jam. Prince and Donna Grantis together on guitars were almost metal-heavy, even when he sang the airy melody of Take Me With U.

Throughout the night, Prince’s instincts were spot-on, hitting the sweet spot of the familiar and deep cuts, and between fidelity to the originals and reinvention to keep songs fresh. He honored every song he played, but he didn’t let any of them overstay their welcome. A couple of verses and choruses of Sign O’ the Times got the point across, while a tearful Nothing Compares 2 U merited every moment. He saved Partyman from the Batman soundtrack, brought out singer Lianne La Havas and New Orleans’ Trombone Shorty to join him for the ballad Sometimes It Snows in April, and finished with the songs he wrote for others in his mid-’80s hot streak, singing The Time’s The Bird and Jungle Love, plus Sheila E.’s The Glamorous Life.

Only his visual sensibility needed fine-tuning as he regularly brought out a dance squad that always seemed amateurish and silly, but nothing stopped the audience from demanding not just an encore but Purple Rain specifically, and Prince obliged. Those enjoying it on their way to the exit scurried back when Prince returned for a second encore, ending the night with the funky Days of Wild.

THE ESSENTIAL AUDIENCE: Friday night with Prince was an event, but Essence spoke directly to African-American women on Saturday, with Mary J. Blige, Jill Scott and Ledisi. It was a night of stories about men, women and relationships, starting with New Orleans-born, Oakland-raised Ledisi. She sang as the sisterly voice of reason: smart, moderate and solution-oriented. Her signature song is Alright, a wise sentiment of acceptance, but it’s not an aspirational message.

Jill Scott was more success-oriented. Dressed for a go-go with straightened hair, a black blouse and black-and-white bell-bottoms, she sang about power and agency. “I’m taking my freedom,” she sang in Golden. She acknowledged hard times but sang not about the challenge but the person who helped her get through it in Put Me Back Together, a song scheduled for her upcoming album.

Scott’s songs are empowerment anthems in words or performance. Before He Loves Me (Lyzel in E Flat), Scott admitted, “I don’t know if I can do this” — the song calls for her to sing operatically, and she has been sick lately. She succeeded as gloriously as you thought she might, but that didn’t diminish the power of the moment and performance.
