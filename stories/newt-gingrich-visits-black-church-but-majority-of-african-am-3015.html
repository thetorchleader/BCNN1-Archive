Newt Gingrich Visits Black Church, But Majority of African-Americans Don’t Think GOP Candidates Are After Their Vote

January 15, 2012 Uncategorized

Broderick Smith says he had never seen a nationally known Republican visit his black community until this week.
Newt Gingrich, one of the GOP presidential hopefuls crisscrossing South Carolina, would receive not-so-friendly questions from some of the residents, said Smith, a 30-year-old African American. But he gave Gingrich credit for visiting the community.
“You don’t see them in our community,” he said of Republicans. “And it’s for a good reason. A lot of their policies hurt our community. Some things we probably could agree on. But we don’t even see them.”
South Carolina Republicans say they have made progress in recent years in attracting more minorities to their party but that their work is not finished.
“We’re more than just trying to reach out to one set,” said Chad Connelly, chairman of the South Carolina Republican Party. “We’re reaching out to all people. We don’t think the principles change just because you are of a different people group.”
The party of Strom Thurmond in 2010 produced U.S. Rep. Tim Scott, the first black Republican congressman in the state in 140 years, and Gov. Nikki Haley, the state’s first female and Indian-American governor.
It remains to be seen what the numbers will be in this year’s presidential primary. In the 2008 GOP presidential primary in South Carolina, less than 2 percent of those voting were minorities. In that year’s Democratic presidential primary in the state, about 56 percent of those voting were minorities.
Edward Cousar, a Rock Hill African American and second vice chairman of the state GOP, said he has seen more minorities join the party in recent years.
“The party is doing the best it can to make inroads,” he said. “It’s just a long process.”
