Hundreds Show Up at Funeral to Mourn Father, 7 Children Who Were Killed In Carbon Monoxide Accident

April 19, 2015 Black

Hundreds on Maryland’s Eastern Shore mourn a father and his seven children, all killed in a carbon monoxide accident.

Seven white caskets, joined by one the color of midnight blue, sat open at the edge of the stage in an auditorium at the University of Maryland Eastern Shore on Saturday afternoon. Seven children and their father.

Hundreds of mourners turned out to remember the family who died of carbon monoxide poisoning about two weeks ago in Princess Anne, Md., some quietly sobbing as they passed by.

But when Tyisha Chambers, the mother of the seven children, arrived at the viewing, her knees buckled and she doubled over in anguish. The mother, tall and thin, wore a white dress. Her oldest son, the oldest of her eight children and the only one still living, held her up.

During the two-hour service, condolences were read, including one sent to the family from President Obama, and speakers recalled Rodney Eric Todd Sr., 36, and the children — the boys Cameron, 13, and Zhi’Heem Todd, 7; and the girls Ty’Juziana, 15, Ty’Keria, 12; Ty’Nijuzia, 11; Ty’Niah, 9; and Ty’Breyia Todd, 5 — whose birthday fell on April 4, two days before she and the others were discovered dead in their home.

The funeral culminated two weeks of heartbreak for relatives of the children and Todd, a University of Maryland Eastern Shore dining-service worker, who was described as a loving father “trying to keep his children warm” with a generator inside the home. “There was nothing that he would not do for them,” his obituary read, “and he always put their needs ahead of his own.”

Investigators said carbon monoxide seeping from the generator killed them as they slept. Police who entered the home April 6, after Todd’s work supervisor reported the family missing, found the generator in the kitchen. The switch was on, and it had run out of gasoline.

Power cords ran from the generator throughout the house, which had been without electricity for two weeks, since Delmarva Power disconnected what company officials described as a “stolen electric meter” attached to the house.

The power company said it was investigating the case but declined to comment further, citing potential litigation.

“Anytime there is an incident with our equipment, we always investigate according to regulatory policy,” Delmarva Power spokesman Matt Likovich said Friday in a statement.

Likovich told The Washington Post that when a utility investigator removed the meter from the house, “no one responded to us being out there as we were removing it or after it was disconnected.” Power company officials said they did not know who stole the meter or attached it to the house on Antioch Avenue.

The meter was removed March 25. The last day anyone saw the family alive was March 27.

Ten days later, the bodies of the children and their father were discovered. Police said the children were found in sleeping positions in their beds. Todd was slumped in the hallway.

Candice Smith, a spokeswoman for Princess Anne Police, said Friday that there is no continuing police investigation into the deaths at the house, which was owned by Gilkerson Properties. Last week, Christopher S. Gilkerson declined to comment on the advice of his attorney.

On Saturday, the little yellow house on Antioch Avenue sat quietly with the windows shut tight as mourners continued to arrive, leaving stuffed animals, toys, and purple and pink balloons on the front porch.

Todd and the children had lived in the house since October. Family members said Todd had been struggling to pay his utility bills.

Todd had been taking care of his seven children since he was released from jail after serving eight months of a 10-year sentence on an assault conviction for stabbing the children’s mother in the face.

Chambers, who lives about 60 miles away in Denton, Md., with her oldest son, 17-year-old Rodney Todd Jr., said she had not lived with her younger children since Todd was released after the 2011 stabbing. She said that after Todd was granted custody, she saw the youngest children on holidays and for their birthdays. She last saw them at Christmastime.

Chambers, 36, a dietary aide at a nursing home in Denton, said she was at work when police discovered the bodies. “It was the worst news somebody could give you,” she said. “I feel numb. It hurts. I feel it’s a horrible dream.”

On Saturday, Chambers, her mother and other family members and friends left Denton in a car caravan to make the 1 1/2 -hour drive to Princess Anne to say a final goodbye to the children.

For their burials, she dressed the five girls in white dresses with silver tiaras and the two boys in white tuxedos with baby blue pocket scarves.

“I wanted to dress them like angels,” she said.

She had asked mourners to wear white and pink to the funeral. And many obliged, lining up early for the viewing and funeral service held in the university’s Ella Fitzgerald Performing Arts Center auditorium.

She wanted the funeral to be a celebration of their short lives.
