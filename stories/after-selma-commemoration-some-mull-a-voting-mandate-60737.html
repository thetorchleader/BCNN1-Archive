After Selma Commemoration, Some Mull a Voting Mandate

March 9, 2015 Black

For all the Voting Rights Act accomplished, President Obama observed in commemorating the 50th anniversary of the Selma march, the United States still had “one of the lowest voting rates among free peoples.”

“How do we so casually discard the right for which so many fought?” he asked at the Edmund Pettus Bridge.

But what if discarding that right wasn’t an option? What if the United States, like Australia among other countries, made voting not a right but an obligation subject to a traffic ticketlike fine if not fulfilled?

Scholars say it could shake up a polarized political system that many Americans have grown to loathe. It would give more Americans at least a tiny personal stake in the democratic process.

Compulsory voting would relieve campaigns of the need to spend vast sums on mobilizing voters, and make persuading the undecided strategically paramount. In turn that could produce a less ideologically strident campaign dialogue. Those pulled into the system by compulsory voting would be those least committed to Republican or Democratic orthodoxy — or, as some conservative critics of expanded voting procedures have put it, “low-information voters.”

So many more voters — four in 10 of those eligible currently sit out presidential contests — would increase the government’s cost of election administration. Requiring their participation would also clash directly with Americans’ instinctive resistance to government mandates.
