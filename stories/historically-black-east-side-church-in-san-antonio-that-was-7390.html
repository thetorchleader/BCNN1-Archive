Historically Black East Side Church in San Antonio that Was Burned Down Will be Rebuilt in a Different Location

June 18, 2013 Uncategorized

Leaders of a historic East Side church destroyed by fire do not plan to rebuild there, opting to relocate to a larger property closer to the suburban addresses of much of its congregation.


Childress Memorial Church of God in Christ on North Pine Street was gutted by fire on Feb. 1. The congregation plans to build a new church on a 25-acre property near Kirby. Photo: John Davenport / San Antonio Express-News
The Feb. 1 blaze caused an estimated $3 million worth of damage to Childress Memorial Church of God in Christ in the Dignowity Hill Historic District, gutting its landmark sanctuary, built in 1908.
In the aftermath, the church’s pastor, Bishop Samuel Iglehart, said he would consider returning but noted he had also been studying relocation in recent years. In the weeks since the fire, he determined the cost to buy adjacent property and the red tape of rebuilding in a historic district were too much, he said.
“It’s not that we didn’t love the place and appreciate the neighborhood,” Iglehart said. “We just couldn’t handle it.”
The church is finalizing the purchase of 25 acres near Kirby where Iglehart plans to build a multipurpose facility to accommodate more than 1,000 people and provide a fitness center and credit union, among other features. Currently, the congregation is renting the Live Oak Civic Center for services.
The historically African American congregation was formed on the East Side in 1931. It is the largest and second-oldest of its denomination in the San Antonio area, with an average weekly attendance of 600-plus, leaders said. A handful of other black churches have relocated from the area in recent years to set up shop closer to suburbia.
The fire prompted an arson investigation with federal resources supporting city officers. It concluded no criminal activity was involved.
Donations from across San Antonio came in to help the congregation rebound, including $100,000 from Community Bible Church and a fundraising service led by St. John Baptist Church.
Iglehart said he believes the new location is in a growing area and has more than enough space for parking and additions, including a possible church-owned housing or apartment development. Since 2004, the congregation has saved about $1 million for expansion plans and expects a sizable insurance claim to help with the move. It backed the idea in a churchwide meeting nearly two weeks ago, he said.
“It motivates people,” Iglehart said. “Everybody is driving by (the new property) and doing special prayers. They’re wanting to see it.”
Any return to its former home would have required a review of exterior designs of new structures by the city’s Office of Historic Preservation and its affiliated commission. And buying and moving houses to add space would face other restrictions designed to discourage altering the character of a historic district.
