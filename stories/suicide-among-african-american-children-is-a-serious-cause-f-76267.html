Suicide Among African-American Children Is a Serious Cause for Concern and a Clarion Call for Action to Be Taken

May 28, 2015 Black

© Jerome Delay/Associated Press A May 18 study led by Jeffrey Bridge, a pediatric expert at Nationwide Children’s Hospital in Columbus, Ohio, found that 657 children aged 5 to 11 have committed suicide since 1993.

Sometimes, children as young as 5 years old respond to trauma and depression in the same way as adults — they commit suicide. But why is the number of African-American children who commit suicide increasing? 

A May 18 study led by Jeffrey Bridge, a pediatric expert at Nationwide Children’s Hospital in Columbus, Ohio, found that 657 children aged 5 to 11 have committed suicide since 1993. Though the rate of suicides remained constant, the rate of African-American boys taking their own lives increased. Suicide rates among white boys in the same age group decreased.

The number of African-American children who killed themselves jumped from 30 in the first five years of the study to 59 by the last five years, with the rate almost doubling to 2.54 per million people.

The study is the first to show a significant racial disparity in suicide rates among any age groups. As a result, study authors recommend that caregivers and social workers spend more time talking with children about suicide, regardless of their race.

“There is a myth that kids under 12 don’t think about suicide,” said study co-author Lisa Horowitz, a clinical psychologist at the National Institute of Mental Health who holds a doctorate in clinical psychology.

“I want pediatricians to start asking about suicide.”

The study, which was published online in JAMA Pediatrics, used national statistics collected by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to analyze the number of children under 11 who took their own lives across four five-year periods between 1993 and 2012.

Joel Greenhouse, a Carnegie Mellon University professor of statistics and study co-author, explained that the variance in suicide rates between African-Americans and whites was notable despite the small sample size. “You always expect some changes from year to year,” he said. “But what we are saying is that this change is more than what you’d expect.”

John Wallace, who has a Ph.D. in sociology and heads the board of the Homewood Children’s Village, said that African-American youth are disproportionately affected by poverty, trauma and community violence, which are risk factors for suicide.

Cheryl Hall-Russell, chief executive officer of the Hill House Association, which provides community services including mental health and home care, said people often do not spot warning signs as easily in African-American boys, because society views them as a threat, regardless of their age.

“If [African-American boys] are down and depressed, they are thought of as having a bad attitude,” Ms. Hall-Russell said. “This is true for boys below the age of 11, as well.”

Mr. Bridge and Ms. Horowitz said the study didn’t attempt to explain racial disparities, but a key takeaway point from the findings is that children as young as 5 should be screened for suicidal thoughts.

Ms. Horowitz said parents don’t expect suicide attempts in young children and are largely unaware that children have even tried to kill themselves. But she said adults in children’s lives should ask them about the issue directly if they notice drastic changes in their moods.
