Big Money Troubles For Michigan School Districts

March 11, 2013 Uncategorized

Only 11 of the 49 Michigan school districts in deficit expect to end the budget year in the black, while 13 have seen their deficits grow during the year, according to a report by State Superintendent Mike Flanagan.

In his quarterly report to the Legislature on Feb. 26, Flanagan updated lawmakers on the financial state of districts that incurred year-end deficits in 2012 and their progress — of lack thereof — in reducing the shortfall during the 2012-13 year.
Overall, Michigan has seen a steady increase in the number of school districts with negative fund balances. In 2001, only 18 of the state’s 549 districts and 256 charters had deficits, but the number has been rising for five years.
Districts unable to resolve their deficits can be placed under the control of an emergency financial manager who has the authority to close schools, lay off teachers and turn an entire district into charter schools. Such changes have taken place in districts under managers such as Detroit Public Schools, Highland Park Schools and Muskegon Heights Schools.
The report showed 25 districts — including Pontiac, Avondale and Oak Park — expect to end the year with smaller deficits. Oak Park schools reported a $5.5 million deficit at the start of the year, but officials cut that figure to a projected $3.55 million at year’s end.
Thirteen districts — including Dearborn Heights, Inkster and Taylor — are expecting to end their budget year with bigger deficits. Inkster found itself with a $12.8 million deficit at the start of the year and said that amount will increase to $15.9 million.
Only 11 districts — including Northpointe Academy, a charter school in Highland Park — informed the state they would emerge from deficit by June 30.
In a letter to school district superintendents Feb. 14, Carol Wolenberg, deputy superintendent for the Michigan Department of Education, urged leaders to take a serious look at where their districts measured up on certain “early warning” indicators of financial stress, such as declining enrollment, increasing staff costs, incurring an operating deficit and a declining fund balance.
“We have seen a marked increase in the number of districts that have experienced a deficit fund balance. The magnitude of some of these deficits seems almost insurmountable,” Wolenberg wrote, adding it’s the responsibility of a district’s board and its administration to “make difficult decisions” to ensure financial viability.
