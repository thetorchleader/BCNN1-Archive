‘Free Tuition’ Doesn’t Quite Solve the College Cost Problem

April 6, 2015 Education

© Ron Lewis/AP Photo Stanford University graduates cheer during the 117th graduation on campus in Palo Alto, Calif., June 15, 2008.

When Stanford University welcomed thousands of new freshmen into the fold late last month, school officials also said they would expand a financial aid program geared toward low- and middle-income students, offering free tuition to those whose families meet certain financial thresholds. But the plan comes at a price to other students and fails to address the core problem of the rising cost of college, experts say. 

The Palo Alto, Calif.-based university on March 27 announced it will amend its financial aid program, which it implemented in 2008, by offering free tuition (set at $45,729 next year) to students who come from families making less than $125,000 annually – up from $100,000. And for those whose family income is below $65,000 – previously $60,000 – the university will pick up the tab for room and board as well, for a total of just under $59,836 next year.

“Our highest priority is that Stanford remain affordable and accessible to the most talented students, regardless of their financial circumstances,” Provost John Etchemendy said in a release. “Our generous financial aid program accomplishes that, and these enhancements will help even more families, including those in the middle class, afford Stanford without going into debt.”

But at the same time, the university’s Board of Trustees has voted to increase tuition by at least 3 percent every year for the last 10 years – or a roughly 47 percent increase since the 2005-06 school year. In doing so, the university funds its financial aid initiative in part from the tuition revenue wealthy student bring in. It also has a $21 billion endowment, and a portion of the returns each year goes toward university operations.

“In general across the country, the big issue is the underlying cost of providing a college education,” says Kevin James, a research fellow at the American Enterprise Institute’s Center on Higher Education Reform. “This is simply shifting money around across students. It’s having some students pay more so others can pay less.”

The plan isn’t unique to Stanford. Most Ivy League schools – including Princeton, Brown, Cornell, Columbia, Harvard, Yale and Dartmouth – have developed some variation of the “free tuition” plan for low and middle income students. And many started doing so around the same time: toward the beginning of or during the Great Recession, when college costs began to skyrocket.

But subsidizing tuition and fees for a group of students isn’t an option for most other schools.

Most universities with those plans in place have also increased their tuition each year for the last several years and have substantial endowments. Some large public universities with larger than average endowments – such as the University of California system – also offer generous financial aid packages to cover tuition for students in need.
